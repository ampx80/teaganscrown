The plain-language version

What a waiver actually is.

Medicaid usually looks at a whole family's income to decide who qualifies. A Home and Community-Based Services waiver, or HCBS waiver, lets a state set some of those rules aside so a child can be covered based on their own disability and needs. The goal is simple and humane: help a person get the support they need to live at home and be part of their community, rather than in an institution.

Every state builds its own waivers, gives them its own names, and decides which services they cover and who is eligible. That is why this tool does not print a single state's waiver names, dollar amounts, or wait times. Those change, and only your state can tell you what is true today. What stays consistent from state to state is the shape of the help and the kind of agency that runs it, and that is what this guide walks you through.

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

What is a Medicaid HCBS waiver?

HCBS stands for Home and Community-Based Services. An HCBS waiver is a Medicaid program that lets states cover supports that help a person live at home and in their community instead of in an institution.

Because it is a waiver, states can set aside some standard Medicaid rules, which often means a child can qualify based on their own needs and disability rather than the whole family's income. Every state designs its own waivers, so names, covered services, and eligibility rules differ from place to place.

What do Medicaid waivers typically help with?

It varies by state and by waiver, but common supports include respite care that gives family caregivers a break, therapies, durable medical equipment and home or vehicle modifications, personal care and help with daily living, service coordination or case management, and community and behavioral supports.

The exact list of covered services, and the limits on each, is set by your state, so always confirm with your state's official program.

Why is there often a waiting list for waivers?

Many HCBS waivers serve a limited number of people at a time, so when demand is higher than the available slots, states keep a waiting list, sometimes called an interest list or registry. Waitlists are common, and how long a family waits can vary a great deal.

Because a spot can open with little notice, most families are encouraged to get on the list as early as possible while also looking into other support programs in the meantime.

How do I find the right agency in my state?

Start with your state Medicaid office and your state's developmental disabilities agency, which usually administers the waivers for children with disabilities. Your state's federally funded Parent Center can also help you understand options at no cost.

This tool links you to authoritative starting points so you can confirm the current, accurate details for where you live.

Is this tool specific legal or eligibility advice?

No. This is a plain-language, general explanation to help you understand how waivers work and where to go next. It does not decide whether your family qualifies, and it does not list any single state's waiver names, amounts, or wait times, because those change and are set by each state.

Always confirm the specifics with your official state Medicaid and developmental disabilities agencies.
