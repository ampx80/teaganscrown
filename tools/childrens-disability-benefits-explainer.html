A plain-language orientation for parents

SSI, Medicaid, ABLE, and waivers, in plain language

Four programs, four sets of rules, and a stack of paperwork that arrives right when you have the least energy to face it. This is a gentle, guided orientation to what each one is and where to go next, so the map feels a little less overwhelming.

Start the walkthrough Explore the four programs

Start here, one word at a time

If you have just learned that your child has a disability, or you are only now coming up for air, the world of benefits can sound like a foreign language. SSI. Medicaid. ABLE. Waivers. Deeming. Resource limits. It is a lot, and no one hands you a translator.

So think of this page as a friendly first read, not a form to fill out. Below, you can tap through the four programs families ask about most and see, in plain words, what each one is and who runs it. Then a short walkthrough builds you a personalized map of official places to start. Nothing here decides anything. The real decisions belong to the agencies, and we point you straight to them.

A gentle heads-up. This is educational information, not legal, medical, or financial advice, and it is not an eligibility decision. Program rules, dollar limits, and waitlists change often and differ by state. Always confirm the details that apply to your family with the official sources linked throughout this page.

Supplemental Security Income

A monthly cash payment

Run by the Social Security Administration (SSA)

SSI is a monthly cash benefit for people who have a qualifying disability and very limited income and resources. Children can qualify too. The money is meant to help with basic needs, and families use it however their child needs, from food and clothing to a share of the rent.

For a child, SSA looks at the child's condition and also at the household's income and resources, a process sometimes called deeming, because a portion of the parents' income is treated as available to the child. That is why some families are surprised one way or the other. When a young person turns 18, SSA generally shifts to looking mainly at that person's own income and resources, which can change the picture entirely.

Who it is for

Children and adults with a qualifying disability and limited income and resources.

What it gives

A monthly cash payment set by SSA, plus, in many states, a door into Medicaid.

Confirm the current rules at SSA.gov

Health coverage

Medical care and services

Run by your state together with the federal government

Medicaid is health coverage. It pays for doctor visits, hospital care, therapies, medications, medical equipment, and much more. It is separate from SSI, run by your state under federal rules, which is why the details differ depending on where you live.

Many children reach Medicaid through their family's income, but there are also pathways based on a child's disability, and in a number of states a child approved for SSI is enrolled in Medicaid automatically or nearly so. Because a single medical bill can dwarf almost anything else in a family's budget, Medicaid is often the most important door to walk through first.

Who it is for

Children and families who meet their state's rules, including disability-based pathways.

What it gives

Coverage for medical care, therapies, equipment, and related services.

Find your state Medicaid office

ABLE accounts

A way to save without losing benefits

Offered by states under a federal law

Here is the quiet problem ABLE accounts were built to solve. Benefits like SSI and Medicaid have strict limits on how much a person can have in savings. For years, that meant families had to keep a child's savings dangerously low or risk losing coverage. An ABLE account changes that.

An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings and investment account for eligible people with disabilities. Money kept in it, up to program limits, does not count against the resource limits for SSI and Medicaid the way an ordinary bank account would. Funds can be used for a broad range of disability-related expenses. There are rules about who is eligible, how much can go in each year, and how the money is used, and those rules can change, so it is worth checking the current details before opening one.

Who it is for

Eligible people whose disability began early in life. Check the current age rule.

What it gives

Protected savings for the future, kept largely separate from benefit resource limits.

Learn more at the ABLE National Resource Center

Home and Community-Based Services

Medicaid waivers

Offered by your state through Medicaid

A waiver is one of the most powerful and least understood tools in the whole system. Through Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers, a state can use Medicaid to pay for supports that help a child live at home and in the community rather than in an institution, things like therapies, respite care, home modifications, or a support worker.

Crucially, many waivers can look only at the child's income rather than the whole family's, which is how some families who earn too much for regular Medicaid still qualify. The catch is that states offer a limited number of slots, so many waivers have waiting lists, sometimes long ones. That is why families are so often told to ask about getting on a list early, even before they are sure they need it.

Who it is for

Children who meet a state's waiver criteria, often based on the child's own income.

What it gives

Funded services and supports for daily life at home and in the community.

Read the HCBS overview at Medicaid.gov

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

Does this tool decide whether my child qualifies for benefits?

No. This is a plain-language orientation, not an eligibility determination. It helps you understand what SSI, Medicaid, ABLE accounts, and waivers are, and which ones may be worth learning more about.

Only the Social Security Administration, your state Medicaid agency, and the official programs themselves can decide eligibility. Always confirm the current rules with those official sources before you rely on anything here.

What is the difference between SSI and Medicaid?

They are two separate programs run by different agencies. SSI, or Supplemental Security Income, is a monthly cash payment from the Social Security Administration for people with disabilities and very limited income and resources, including children. Medicaid is health coverage, run by your state together with the federal government, that pays for medical care and services.

In many states a child who is approved for SSI is also enrolled in Medicaid, but the two are applied for and decided separately.

Will opening an ABLE account make my child lose their benefits?

ABLE accounts are specifically designed so that money saved in them, up to program limits, does not count against the resource limits for SSI and Medicaid the way ordinary savings would. That is the whole point of an ABLE account.

There are rules about who is eligible, how much can be contributed, and how funds can be used, and those rules can change. Check the ABLE National Resource Center and your state's ABLE program for the current details before you open one.

What is a Medicaid waiver, and why do people talk about waitlists?

A waiver, often a Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waiver, lets a state use Medicaid to pay for supports that help a child live at home and in the community, such as therapies, respite, or a support worker. Many waivers can look only at the child's own income rather than the whole family's, which is why some families who earn too much for regular Medicaid can still qualify.

Because states offer a limited number of slots, many waivers have waiting lists, so it is common advice to ask about applying early. Rules and lists vary by state.

Do I need a lawyer or paid service to apply?

No. You can apply directly with the Social Security Administration for SSI and with your state Medicaid agency, and it is free to do so. Free help is available too. Every state has a federally funded Parent Center that offers no-cost guidance, and many communities have benefits counselors.

This page is educational and is not legal or financial advice, so for decisions about your specific situation, lean on those official and free resources.

Where should I actually start?

A common starting order is to contact the Social Security Administration about SSI, contact your state Medicaid agency about coverage and any waivers, look into an ABLE account through the ABLE National Resource Center if you want to save for your child's future, and reach out to your state Parent Center for free, patient guidance along the way.
