Flying With a Child and Medical Equipment

Yes, you can do this - here is how to move your child, their gear, and your own nerves safely from curb to seat, one calm step at a time.

Your Timeline: Curb to Seat

1
4 to 6 weeks out - call the airline's disability deskNot the regular reservation line - ask for the special assistance or medical desk. Confirm your child's device (oxygen concentrator, ventilator, feeding pump) is approved and request any medical clearance form (often called a MEDIF). Oxygen and ventilators almost always need paperwork; wheelchairs usually do not.
2
2 weeks out - gather the paper and the suppliesGet a signed doctor's letter listing diagnoses, devices, and medications. Refill prescriptions early. Order roughly double the supplies you expect to need (extra G-tube, trach ties, syringes, batteries). Print every document twice.
3
72 hours out - call TSA CaresReach (855) 787-2227 and describe your child's needs and flight. They can arrange help at the checkpoint so you are not improvising with a line building behind you.
4
The night before - build the carry-onPack all essential medications and at least a day of supplies in your carry-on - never check them. Fully charge every battery. Attach gate-check tags and handling notes to the wheelchair or stroller.
5
At the airport - ask for preboardingRequest preboarding so you have time to transfer, stow gear, and settle before the aisle fills. Hand the agent your doctor's letter if asked. Use a TSA notification card at security to explain quietly.
6
At the gate - protect the chairGate-check the wheelchair or adaptive stroller at the door of the plane, not at baggage. Remove and carry on any fragile parts (headrest, joystick, cushion) and photograph the device before it leaves your hands.

Your rights are stronger than you think

Air travel with a disability in the United States is governed by the Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA). Under it, your child's mobility and medical equipment - manual and power wheelchairs, walkers, gait trainers, standers that fold, adaptive strollers used as wheelchairs - travels free and does not count against your baggage allowance or carry-on limit. That is the law, not a courtesy, and it applies on every US airline.

Every airline must also have a Complaint Resolution Official (CRO) on duty whenever it operates. If a gate agent tells you something that sounds wrong - that the chair counts as a bag, that you cannot preboard, that your child's feeding pump is not allowed - politely ask to speak with the CRO. They are trained in the ACAA and can override a mistaken frontline call on the spot.

Knowing the rule by name changes the conversation. You are not asking for a favor. You are asking the airline to follow a federal regulation it already knows.

Liquids, formula, and medication get a pass

The 3-1-1 liquids rule - the one that stops you bringing a full water bottle through security - does not apply to medically necessary items. Formula, breast milk, expressed milk, liquid tube-feeding formula, thickened liquids, and both liquid and gel medications are all allowed through the checkpoint in reasonable quantities, even over 3.4 ounces.

You do not need them in a zip bag, but you do need to declare them. Tell the TSA officer at the start of screening: 'I have medically necessary liquids and my child's medications to declare.' Officers may test containers or ask to inspect them, and you can request they change gloves and use a fresh area if your child is medically fragile. Ice packs, gel packs, and freezer packs to keep formula or meds cold are allowed too, even if they have partly melted.

Keep medications in original labeled packaging when you can - it prevents questions - and carry a printed medication list. Pack a full trip's worth of meds plus a few extra days in your carry-on, because checked bags get lost and pharmacies at your destination may not stock what your child needs.

Oxygen and ventilators: the paperwork devices

Compressed and liquid oxygen tanks are generally not permitted in the cabin, and airlines do not provide oxygen for personal use during the flight. What you can bring is a portable oxygen concentrator (POC) - but only FAA-approved models. Check that your specific unit is on the approved list, notify the airline in advance, and bring enough fully charged batteries to run it for about 150 percent of your total door-to-door travel time, counting delays and layovers.

Home ventilators, CPAP and BiPAP machines, portable suction, and feeding pumps are all allowed onboard as assistive devices, and they do not count as carry-on baggage. Ventilators in particular usually require you to coordinate with the airline's medical desk ahead of time and may need a physician's form. These are the devices worth calling about six weeks out, not three days out.

A device that runs on a lithium battery generally has to travel in the cabin with you, not in checked baggage. Bring the charger, bring spares where allowed, and label everything with your name and phone number.

Protecting the wheelchair (the thing you cannot replace)

For most families the wheelchair is the highest-stakes item on the trip - custom-fitted, expensive, and irreplaceable in a day. Gate-check it, meaning you use it all the way down the jet bridge and hand it over at the aircraft door, and you get it back at the door on arrival rather than at baggage claim. Ask for a gate-check tag specifically.

Before you let it go, take photos from several angles to document its condition, and remove any parts that can be lost or crushed: headrest, seat cushion, joystick, lateral supports, and footplates travel with you in the cabin. Tape a laminated card to the frame with handling instructions - how to fold it, how to disengage the motor, what not to lift by - because the people loading it have never seen your child's chair before.

Power wheelchairs have extra battery rules depending on whether they use sealed, wet, or lithium cells, so tell the airline the battery type when you book. If the chair comes back damaged, report it before you leave the airport and ask for the CRO - under the ACAA the airline is responsible for repair or replacement of a wheelchair it damages.

Boarding, seating, and surviving the flight

Always take the offer of preboarding. Those few extra minutes let you transfer your child, stow equipment, wipe down the tray, and get organized before a plane full of people is waiting behind you. If your child cannot walk down the aisle, request an aisle chair (a narrow boarding chair the airline provides) when you book and again at the gate.

Think through the seat itself. A bulkhead row gives more floor space for feet, feeding pumps, or a tucked-away device, though the tray and entertainment screen may fold out of the armrest. For a small child who needs restraint, the CARES harness is the only FAA-approved harness-type device for children roughly 22 to 44 pounds; larger or differently-abled children may need a doctor's guidance on positioning. Ask the airline what fits your child.

Bring comfort and regulation items in easy reach: noise-reducing headphones, a familiar chewy or fidget, a preferred blanket, the AAC device fully charged. Cabin pressure changes, engine noise, and crowding are a lot for any child and more for a child with sensory needs. You are allowed to board first, dim expectations, and go slow.

When the trip is bigger than the ticket

The financial and logistical weight of a medical trip is real, and families rarely have to carry it alone. Some hospitals have travel or social-work programs, and organizations exist to help families reach specialized care. If you are flying for treatment, ask your child's care team and hospital social worker what travel assistance they know of - they often have a list you will never find on your own.

Frequently asked questions

Do I really not have to pay to bring my son's wheelchair?
Correct. Under the Air Carrier Access Act, mobility and assistive devices - wheelchairs, walkers, gait trainers, adaptive strollers used as wheelchairs - are transported free and do not count as baggage on any US airline. If an agent says otherwise, politely ask for the Complaint Resolution Official.
Can I bring my daughter's tube-feeding formula and thickened liquids through security?
Yes. Medically necessary liquids, including tube-feed formula, breast milk, and thickened liquids, are exempt from the 3.4-ounce limit and allowed in reasonable quantities. Just tell the TSA officer you have medically necessary liquids to declare at the start of screening. Cooling packs to keep them cold are allowed too.
My child uses oxygen. Can the airline just supply it?
Generally no - airlines do not provide oxygen for personal use, and compressed or liquid oxygen tanks are usually not allowed in the cabin. You bring an FAA-approved portable oxygen concentrator with enough charged batteries for about 150 percent of your total travel time, and you notify the airline in advance. Call the airline's medical desk four to six weeks out.
How do I make security less overwhelming for my child?
Call TSA Cares at (855) 787-2227 at least 72 hours before your flight; they can arrange assistance at the checkpoint. You can also print a free TSA notification card to hand the officer, request a private screening, and ask officers to change gloves. Your child does not have to be separated from a needed device or from you.
Will my child's power wheelchair get damaged?
It can happen, which is why you gate-check it at the aircraft door, photograph it first, remove fragile parts to carry on, and tape handling instructions to the frame. Tell the airline the battery type when you book. If it comes back damaged, report it before leaving the airport - the airline is responsible for repair or replacement under the ACAA.
Should we preboard or wait so my child sits for less time?
Preboard. The extra minutes to transfer your child, stow equipment, and settle before the crowd are worth far more than the shorter sit. If your child cannot walk the aisle, request an aisle chair when you book and again at the gate. Bring headphones, a fidget, and comfort items for the wait once seated.
