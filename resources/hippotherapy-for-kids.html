Hippotherapy: What It Is and How to Access It

A horse's walk moves your child's body in ways a therapy table never can, and here is exactly how to find it, qualify for it, and help pay for it.

So what actually is hippotherapy?

Picture your child's regular physical, occupational, or speech therapy, but the treatment table is a warm, patient horse and the ground keeps gently moving. That is hippotherapy. The word comes from the Greek 'hippos' (horse), and the whole idea rests on one beautiful fact: a walking horse produces a rhythmic, repetitive, three-dimensional movement that is remarkably similar to the motion of a human pelvis while walking.

For a child who cannot walk, or who has low trunk control, or who processes the world differently, that borrowed movement is powerful. The horse does the walking; your child's brain and body do the responding, adjusting, balancing, and organizing, hundreds of times per session, without it ever feeling like work.

Here is the part families most need to hear clearly: hippotherapy is a treatment tool, not a standalone service and not a horseback riding lesson. It is used inside occupational therapy, physical therapy, or speech-language therapy, delivered by a licensed and credentialed therapist. The correct way professionals now describe it is 'OT, PT, or speech therapy incorporating hippotherapy.'

Why a horse, of all things?

A therapy horse gives your child inputs no clinic tool can replicate. The gait delivers roughly a hundred rhythmic movement impulses per minute straight into the pelvis and spine, so muscles fire and adjust constantly. The warmth and width of the horse's back can relax tight legs and open the hips. And the height, the smell, the sway, and the connection with a living animal light up motivation in a way flashcards rarely do.

Therapists use these effects on purpose. They change the horse's speed, direction, and even the child's position (sitting forward, backward, lying prone) to target specific goals: head and trunk control, postural symmetry, balance reactions, breath support for speech, sensory regulation, and attention. For a child who dreads the therapy room, the horse can turn a hard hour into the best part of the week.

Hippotherapy is NOT therapeutic riding (this matters for funding)

This is the single most common mix-up, and getting it straight will save you time with both insurers and grant programs. Hippotherapy is medical treatment: a licensed therapist, a physician referral, individual goals, hands-on guidance, and progress notes. Your child usually does not steer the horse and riding skill is not the point.

Therapeutic or adaptive riding is different, and wonderful in its own right. It is a recreational, adaptive sport taught by a certified riding instructor (often PATH Intl. certified), where your child learns to ride and control the horse. It builds confidence, joy, and community, but it is not billed as therapy and generally is not covered by health insurance.

Many families end up doing both over time. Just know which one you are asking for: say 'therapy incorporating hippotherapy' when you want a clinical, potentially insurable service, and 'therapeutic riding' when you want adaptive recreation.

Could my child be a candidate?

Therapists have used hippotherapy with children who have cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, autism, developmental delay, sensory processing differences, and other neuromotor conditions. The common thread is a therapy goal (postural control, coordination, sensory regulation, communication) that the horse's movement can help address.

It is not right for everyone, and safety comes first. Certain conditions can be cautions or contraindications, such as unstable spines, uncontrolled seizures, severe osteoporosis, or recent surgeries. This is exactly why an in-person evaluation exists. For a child facing spinal fusion, for example, timing around surgery is a real conversation to have with both the surgeon and the therapist.

The honest answer to 'is this for us?' is: ask a credentialed therapist who can see your child. A good one will tell you plainly if it is a fit, and will not pressure you if it is not.

Will insurance cover it? The honest picture

Sometimes, and it depends heavily on your plan. Because hippotherapy is a tool inside therapy, there is no special 'hippotherapy' billing code. Clinics bill the standard occupational, physical, or speech therapy CPT codes, the same codes used in any clinic. When it works, coverage flows because the service is medically necessary skilled therapy that happens to use a horse.

The snags are real. Medicare has no national coverage determination for hippotherapy, and some commercial plans have labeled it experimental or investigational in their policies. A generic 'equine therapy' S-code is often not recognized. So two families with the same diagnosis can get two different answers depending on their insurer and state.

For children, your strongest coverage tool is often Medicaid. Under the EPSDT benefit (Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment), state Medicaid programs must cover medically necessary services for kids, which can include the OT, PT, or speech therapy that incorporates hippotherapy. A home and community-based waiver may also help. Rules vary by state, so verify before you assume.

If insurance says no: how families actually pay for it

Do not stop at the first denial, and do not assume the cash price is your only option. Most families cover hippotherapy by stacking sources. Start with the therapy center itself: many are nonprofits with sliding-scale fees, scholarship funds, or donor-sponsored sessions. Ask directly, 'Do you have financial assistance or a scholarship program?'

Then look at medical-need grants that fund therapies and equipment for children. The UnitedHealthcare Children's Foundation, First Hand Foundation, and Variety (the Children's Charity) all fund child health-related needs; eligibility and covered items vary, so read each program's current guidelines. Local civic groups, foundations, and faith communities sometimes sponsor sessions too.

Finally, use the accounts you may already have. An HSA or FSA can often pay for medically necessary therapy, an ABLE account can hold disability-related savings without risking benefits, and qualifying out-of-pocket medical costs may be tax-deductible. When insurance denies a covered code, file the appeal: a therapist's letter of medical necessity plus progress notes is your best evidence.

What a session really looks like

You will not see your child galloping off. A typical session is calm and deliberate. A trained horse handler leads the horse, one or two side-walkers keep your child safe, and the licensed therapist works hands-on the entire time, adjusting position and challenge minute to minute. Sessions often run about 30 to 45 minutes, because that borrowed movement is genuinely hard work for the body.

Dress your child in long pants and closed-toe shoes, bring any orthotics, and expect happy tiredness afterward. Most importantly, expect it to look like therapy, with written goals and progress notes, not like a pony ride. That clinical structure is exactly what makes it effective, and exactly what keeps it fundable.

Get the CPT codes BEFORE your first session

Ask the clinic exactly which occupational, physical, or speech therapy CPT codes they will bill, then call your insurer and ask if those codes are covered when delivered in an equine setting. Get a reference number for the call. This five-minute step prevents the gut-punch of a surprise 'experimental, not covered' denial after you have already fallen in love with the progress.

Frequently asked questions

Is hippotherapy the same as horseback riding lessons?
No. Hippotherapy is skilled medical therapy delivered by a licensed OT, PT, or speech therapist who uses the horse's movement as a treatment tool, usually without your child steering. Riding lessons (therapeutic or adaptive riding) are recreation taught by a riding instructor. They can both be great, but only the therapy version is billed as medical care.
How do I find a qualified provider I can trust?
Look for a licensed OT, PT, or SLP with hippotherapy training, at a center with well-trained therapy horses. The American Hippotherapy Association keeps a 'Find a Professional' directory, and PATH Intl. lists member centers. Cross-check both, then ask the clinic directly about the therapist's license, credentials, and safety practices (handler plus side-walkers).
Does Medicaid cover hippotherapy for children?
It can. Under the EPSDT benefit, Medicaid must cover medically necessary services for kids, which can include OT, PT, or speech therapy that incorporates hippotherapy, and a waiver may add options. Coverage is decided by medical necessity and varies by state and program, so confirm with your state Medicaid or managed-care plan before starting.
My insurer called it experimental and denied it. Now what?
That denial is common but not the end. Ask the clinic to bill standard therapy CPT codes, request a letter of medical necessity from the therapist, and file an appeal with progress notes attached. In parallel, ask the center about scholarships or sliding-scale fees and apply to grant programs like UnitedHealthcare Children's Foundation, First Hand Foundation, or Variety.
Is it safe for a child who is nonverbal or uses equipment?
Often yes, with the right team and an individualized evaluation. Trained programs use a horse handler and side-walkers, and the therapist adapts positioning for your child's needs. Some conditions (unstable spine, uncontrolled seizures, recent surgery) can be cautions or contraindications, which is exactly why an in-person evaluation and clearance from your child's doctor come first.
How much does it cost out of pocket?
It varies widely by region, provider, and whether insurance pays. Because there is no fixed national price and many providers offer scholarships or sliding-scale fees, the honest move is to ask the specific center for their private-pay rate and financial assistance options, then check what your insurance will cover before you commit.
