Pennsylvania family guide

Medicaid waivers for children with disabilities in Pennsylvania

A plain-language orientation to home and community-based waivers - what they do, who they generally help, and how families usually begin. Then we point you to the official Pennsylvania sources for the specifics.

Apply for help Official resources

If you are raising a child with a disability in Pennsylvania, you have probably heard the word "waiver" and felt your stomach tighten. It sounds like paperwork and gatekeeping. In reality, a Medicaid waiver is one of the most important doors a family can walk through, because it can help pay for the kind of everyday support that keeps a child growing up at home, with the people who love them. This page is a calm, general orientation. It is not legal advice, and it will not give you numbers that change from year to year. What it will do is help you understand the landscape and know where to go next.

What a home and community-based waiver actually does

In Pennsylvania, Medicaid is called Medical Assistance. A home and community-based services waiver - often shortened to HCBS waiver - is a program that "waives" some of the ordinary Medical Assistance rules. That waiving is the whole point. It lets a person receive long-term services and supports in their own home and community instead of in an institution.

For a child, that can mean support that regular health insurance simply was not built to cover: help with daily care, coordination of services, family training, respite so caregivers can rest, and community participation so a child can be part of ordinary life. Each waiver defines its own list of covered services, its own eligibility rules, and its own limits. Because those details are set by the program and can change, this page describes them in general terms and sends you to the official source to confirm what applies to your child right now.

The one idea to hold onto

A waiver is not charity and it is not a favor. It is a program your child may have a right to qualify for. The job in front of you is to find out which program fits, get your child formally determined eligible, and get their name in line. Everything else follows from those steps.

Who these programs generally help

Pennsylvania runs several waiver and Medical Assistance programs, and different ones serve different groups of children and adults. Broadly, families of children with disabilities tend to look into supports for:

  • Children with intellectual disability or autism, where the state's developmental programs office and county offices coordinate services and eligibility.
  • Children who are medically complex or medically fragile, who may need substantial care to remain safely at home.
  • Children with significant physical disabilities who need long-term supports and services.

An important note that many families do not hear early enough: some Medical Assistance pathways for children with disabilities look at the child's own situation rather than only at the whole household's income. That is why you should not assume your family "earns too much" and quietly give up. The rules are specific, they are not the same for every program, and the only way to know is to ask the official source directly.

Why there is often a waiting list

Waiver programs are funded to serve a set number of people, so many use an interest list or waiting list. This is one of the hardest parts for families to sit with, and it is also the clearest argument for starting early. Getting your child registered and formally determined eligible is what puts them in line. A child who has not yet started the process is not on any list at all. If the size of the task feels overwhelming, remember that the first step is usually just a phone call or an online application, not a finished stack of documents.

How families typically begin the process

There is no single magic form, but the path usually starts with two conversations happening around the same time.

1. Apply for Medical Assistance

Families generally apply for Medical Assistance for the child. Pennsylvania offers an online application system called COMPASS, and you can also apply through your County Assistance Office. This establishes the Medicaid foundation that waiver services are built on.

2. Contact the county program for your child's needs

At the same time, families reach out to the county office that handles services for intellectual disability and autism, or for medically complex needs, depending on the child. This local office is often the most useful phone call you will make. They can explain which waiver or program fits, how eligibility is determined, and what enrollment looks like. In many cases a service coordinator will help walk your family through the steps.

3. Gather what documents your child's situation calls for

Every program asks for its own documentation, which may include records of your child's diagnosis, needs, and daily support. Rather than guessing, ask the county office or the Department of Human Services for the current checklist so you are working from an accurate list instead of a rumor.

You do not have to understand the entire system to take the first step. You only have to make the first call.

A word about denials and appeals

Sometimes an application is denied, or a family is told a service is not covered, and it turns out the decision can be questioned. You have the right to understand a decision and, in many cases, to appeal it. You do not need to hire a lawyer to begin. Free legal-aid and health-law organizations in Pennsylvania help families understand Medical Assistance decisions at no cost, and we have listed a couple of them below. Asking for help is not a sign that you are doing this wrong. It is how experienced families get through it.

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

What is a Medicaid waiver in Pennsylvania?

In Pennsylvania, Medicaid is called Medical Assistance. A home and community-based waiver is a program that waives certain regular Medicaid rules so a person can receive long-term services and supports at home and in the community rather than in an institution.

For children with disabilities, waivers can help cover supports that ordinary health insurance usually does not, so a child can grow up with their family.

Does my income disqualify my child?

Not necessarily. Some Medical Assistance pathways for children with disabilities look at the child's own situation rather than only the household's income.

Because eligibility rules are specific and can change, do not assume your family earns too much. Confirm your child's options directly with the Pennsylvania Department of Human Services or a county program before ruling anything out.

Is there a waiting list for waivers?

Waiver programs serve a limited number of people, so many use interest lists or waiting lists. Getting your child registered and formally determined eligible is what puts them in line, so families are often encouraged to start the process early rather than wait.

Check current status with the Department of Human Services or your county office.

How do I start the process for my child?

Families generally begin two conversations at once: applying for Medical Assistance for the child, often through the state's COMPASS system, and contacting the county program that handles services for intellectual disability, autism, or medically complex needs.

The county office explains which waiver or program fits and walks you through eligibility and enrollment.

What kinds of support can a waiver help with?

Depending on the specific waiver and your child's assessed needs, supports may include things like service coordination, in-home help, respite for caregivers, therapies, assistive technology, and community participation.

The exact services, limits, and amounts are defined by each program, so confirm details with the official program rather than assuming what is covered.

Do I need a lawyer to apply?

No. Many families apply on their own with help from a county service coordinator.

If you run into a denial or a complicated eligibility question, free legal-aid and health-law organizations in Pennsylvania can help you understand your rights and next steps at no cost.
