Start here - the one word that unlocks funding

Insurers and Medicaid rarely use the word 'AAC.' The magic phrase is 'speech-generating device,' or SGD. It is the billing category that turns a communication device - including many eye-gaze systems - into covered durable medical equipment (DME). When you call your plan, ask specifically about coverage for a speech-generating device and the evaluation that goes with it.

AAC simply means augmentative and alternative communication - any tool that helps a person communicate when speech alone is not enough. That ranges from picture boards to a dedicated tablet with a voice, to an eye-gaze device your child controls just by looking. The path to funding is similar for all of them, and it almost always starts with an evaluation.

Step 1: The AAC evaluation is your golden ticket

Before anyone pays for a device, they want proof it is the right one. A formal AAC evaluation - usually done by a speech-language pathologist (SLP), sometimes with an occupational therapist for eye-gaze positioning - produces exactly that. The report documents what your child can do, which access method works (touch, switch, or eye-gaze), and why a specific device is medically necessary.

This evaluation is often covered by insurance or Medicaid itself, and many device manufacturers loan out equipment for a home trial during the process. Do not skip it or try to buy a device on your own first - nearly every insurer, waiver, and grant program will ask for this document, and having it ready makes every later step faster.

Step 2: Insurance and Medicaid before anything else

Charitable grants are wonderful, but almost all of them expect you to try insurance first. For children, your strongest tool is often Medicaid. Under the EPSDT benefit (Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment), state Medicaid programs must cover services and equipment that are medically necessary to correct or improve a child's condition - and a speech-generating device frequently qualifies. EPSDT applies to enrolled children under 21.

If your child has private insurance, submit the SGD as DME with the evaluation and a letter of medical necessity. Many families have both private insurance and Medicaid; Medicaid can act as secondary coverage and pick up costs the primary plan leaves behind. Coverage rules and device lists vary by state and plan, so ask for the specific criteria in writing.

Step 3: A denial is not the end - it is a step

Getting denied on the first try is common, and it is often reversible. Ask for the denial in writing, read the stated reason, and file an appeal. Your appeal is strongest when it includes the AAC evaluation, a letter of medical necessity from the prescribing clinician, and plain-language notes on how the device meets your child's daily needs.

You usually have a limited window to appeal, so note any deadline the letter gives you. Your child's SLP and the device manufacturer's funding team deal with these appeals constantly and will often help you assemble the paperwork at no charge - ask them.

Step 4: Real grant programs that help with the gap

When insurance leaves a copay, denies the claim, or moves too slowly, charitable grants can bridge the gap. The UnitedHealthcare Children's Foundation offers grants for medical needs not fully covered by a family's commercial health plan. First Hand Foundation helps children with out-of-pocket costs for medical equipment. Variety - the Children's Charity has chapters that fund communication devices and adaptive equipment in many regions.

Each program has its own eligibility rules, income guidelines, and application steps, and funding is never guaranteed. Read requirements carefully, and apply to more than one - families often stack a grant on top of partial insurance coverage rather than relying on a single source.

Step 5: Borrow, trial, and crowdfund the rest

Every state has an Assistive Technology Act program, and many run device lending libraries or 'loan closets' where you can borrow AAC and eye-gaze equipment to trial for free before committing. This is a great way to confirm a device works for your child and to have something to use while funding is pending. Search for your state's AT Act program to find the nearest lending library.

If a balance remains, thoughtful crowdfunding can close it. Be concrete about the device, the total cost, and what insurance already covered - specific, honest campaigns tend to do far better than open-ended ones.

Say 'speech-generating device,' not 'AAC,' on the phone

When you call your insurer or Medicaid, ask about a 'speech-generating device' (SGD) billed as durable medical equipment. That exact wording routes your request to the right coverage category and can save weeks of being told 'we don't cover that.'

Frequently asked questions

Does Medicaid really cover eye-gaze and AAC devices for kids?
Often, yes. For children under 21, Medicaid's EPSDT benefit covers services and equipment that are medically necessary, and speech-generating devices frequently qualify. The specific criteria and device lists vary by state, so ask your state Medicaid program for its coverage rules in writing.
My insurance denied the device. What do I do?
Do not panic - first denials are common and often reversible. Request the denial in writing, note the reason and any appeal deadline, and file an appeal with your child's AAC evaluation and a letter of medical necessity. Your SLP and the device manufacturer's funding team can usually help you appeal at no cost.
Can I get a device to try before all the funding comes through?
Yes. Many device manufacturers loan equipment for a home trial during the evaluation, and your state's AT Act program may run a lending library where you can borrow AAC or eye-gaze devices for free. This lets your child start communicating while paperwork is in process.
Do I need an evaluation, or can I just buy a device?
Get the evaluation first. A formal AAC or speech-generating-device evaluation documents that a specific device is medically necessary, and nearly every insurer, waiver, and grant program requires it. Buying on your own can actually make funding harder to obtain later.
Which grant should I apply to first?
Apply to insurance or Medicaid first, since most grants expect it. For gaps and copays, reputable programs include the UnitedHealthcare Children's Foundation, First Hand Foundation, and Variety. Eligibility and funding vary, so it is smart to apply to more than one rather than counting on a single source.
How long does the whole process take?
It varies widely - some families are approved in a few weeks, others navigate appeals over several months. That is exactly why trialing a loaner device early matters, so your child has a voice while the funding steps play out.
