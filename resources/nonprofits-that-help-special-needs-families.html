Start Here: Nonprofits Are Usually Step Four, Not Step One

When a family is staring at a $6,000 quote for a gait trainer or a communication device, the instinct is to search 'grants' immediately. That instinct is right - but the order matters. Most nonprofits are designed to fill the gap after insurance and public benefits, not to replace them. In fact, many grant programs specifically ask for a copy of your insurance denial before they will fund an item.

So the fastest path to a 'yes' usually starts with a prescription and an insurance claim, even if you expect it to be denied. That paper trail is not busywork - it is the exact documentation that unlocks the nonprofit money later. Think of it as building a file: prescription, quote from the supplier, denial letter, and a letter of medical necessity from your child's doctor. With those four things in hand, you become one of the easiest families for a grant reviewer to say yes to.

The Big Public Programs Behind the Nonprofits

Before the private organizations, know the public backbone. Medicaid's EPSDT benefit (Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic and Treatment) requires state Medicaid programs to cover medically necessary services for eligible children under 21 - often including equipment and therapies that adult Medicaid would not. Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers can provide respite, in-home support, and equipment, though many states keep waitlists, so it is worth getting on the list early even if you are unsure you qualify.

SSI (Supplemental Security Income) provides monthly income for children with qualifying disabilities in lower-income households, and in most states SSI approval also opens the door to Medicaid. Early Intervention (birth to age three) and school-based IEP or 504 supports round out the public safety net. None of these are 'nonprofits,' but they are the foundation - and the more of this you have in place, the less the private grants have to stretch.

National Nonprofits That Fund Equipment and Care

A handful of well-run national organizations quietly fund equipment and treatment for kids every year. The UnitedHealthcare Children's Foundation offers medical grants to help families with commercially insured children cover costs their plan does not, such as therapies and equipment. First Hand Foundation (funded by Cerner/Oracle Health) helps with clinically related needs for children, including devices, therapy, and sometimes vehicle or home modifications.

Variety - the Children's Charity operates through regional chapters and has long funded adaptive bikes, wheelchairs, communication devices, and mobility equipment, though what each chapter offers varies. Because eligibility, funding cycles, and covered items differ by organization and can change year to year, always read the current guidelines on each group's own website rather than trusting a static list. The good news: applying is free, and applying to more than one is not only allowed but wise.

Diagnosis-Based and Local Help You Might Miss

Some of the most generous help is tied to a specific diagnosis. National condition organizations - for cerebral palsy, spina bifida, Down syndrome, rare diseases, and many others - frequently run family grant funds, equipment programs, or connect you to local chapters with their own resources. If your child has a named diagnosis, search that organization's name plus 'family assistance' or 'grants.'

Local giving is often faster and less bureaucratic than national programs. Many communities have a Knights of Columbus, Kiwanis, Lions Club, Elks Lodge, or Shriners presence that funds equipment or covers care for a child in need, sometimes within weeks. Your children's hospital social worker is a powerful ally here - they usually keep a working list of the local funds that actually pay out, and they know which ones respond quickly.

Loan Closets and AT Programs: Free Gear Right Now

Not every need calls for a grant. Under the federal Assistive Technology (AT) Act, every state and territory runs an AT program, and many operate device loan closets and reuse programs where you can borrow or receive gently used equipment - walkers, standers, communication devices, shower chairs - at little or no cost. This is ideal when you need something now, or want to trial a device before committing to a specific model.

Local independent living centers, Muscular Dystrophy Association chapters, and disease-specific loan closets can also lend equipment. The trade-off is availability: you take what is in stock, and sizing matters for growing kids. But for a family in a pinch - or one waiting on a grant decision - a loan closet can bridge the gap for free while the paperwork works its way through.

How to Actually Apply Without Losing Your Mind

Grant applications reward preparation. Build one folder (paper or digital) with your child's diagnosis, the prescription, the supplier's itemized quote, your insurance denial, proof of income if requested, and a short story in your own words about how this item will change your child's daily life. That last piece matters more than families realize - reviewers are human, and a specific, concrete paragraph ('a gait trainer would let Teagan walk to the table for dinner with her family') lands harder than a form.

Then apply to several programs at once rather than waiting for each answer. Approvals can take weeks to months, funding cycles open and close, and a 'no' from one is not a 'no' from all. Keep a simple tracking list of where you applied and when, and follow up politely. Persistence, not perfection, is what gets these funded.

Watch Out for the Fakes

One hard rule protects families from the most common scam: a legitimate grant or nonprofit will never ask you to pay a fee to apply or to 'release' your funds. If an organization wants a processing payment, a gift card, or your bank login before it will help, walk away. Real programs are funded by donors, hospitals, and foundations - the whole point is that the money flows to you, not from you.

Be equally cautious with 'free money' ads and pressure to act immediately. Verify any organization by finding it independently through a search or your hospital social worker, and check that a charity is real (many families use a charity rating site) before sharing documents. The trustworthy programs in this guide will still be there tomorrow - urgency is a red flag, not a reason to hurry.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to be low-income to get help?
Not always. Some programs, like SSI, are income-based, but many equipment and diagnosis-based grants consider medical need and unmet cost rather than income alone. And programs like the UnitedHealthcare Children's Foundation are aimed at families who have commercial insurance. Read each program's guidelines - do not assume you earn too much to qualify.
Can I apply to more than one nonprofit at the same time?
Yes, and you should. Applying to several programs at once is normal and smart, because funding cycles, budgets, and covered items differ, and a decline from one does not predict the others. Keep a simple list of where and when you applied so you can follow up.
How long do grants take?
It varies widely - some local funds respond in days, while national programs can take weeks to months and may fund in cycles. Because timing is unpredictable, apply early, apply to multiple sources, and consider a loan closet to cover the need in the meantime.
Is it a scam if they ask for a fee?
Yes. A legitimate nonprofit or grant will never charge you to apply or ask for payment, gift cards, or your bank login to 'release' funds. If money is expected to flow from you before you receive help, stop and verify the organization independently.
What if my insurance denied the equipment?
A denial is often the beginning, not the end. Ask your child's doctor for a letter of medical necessity and file an appeal - many first-round denials for durable medical equipment are overturned. Meanwhile, that denial letter also qualifies you for many nonprofit grants.
Who can help me find the right programs for my child?
Your children's hospital or clinic social worker is often the best starting point - they keep current lists of local funds that actually pay out. Your state's Assistive Technology program, Early Intervention office, and diagnosis-specific organizations are also strong, free resources.
