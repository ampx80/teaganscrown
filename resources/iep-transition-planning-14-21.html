IEP Transition Planning, Ages 14 to 21

Transition planning turns the IEP from "what happens this year" into a real roadmap toward adult life - work, learning, and as much independence as your child can reach.

By 16 (14 in many states)
Federal IDEA requires transition planning in the IEP by age 16; states like Pennsylvania start at 14
3 goal areas
Every plan needs measurable postsecondary goals for education/training, employment, and (when needed) independent living
Age 18
In most states, IEP rights transfer to your child at the age of majority unless you plan ahead
Through 21 or 22
IDEA services continue until a regular diploma or the state's exit age - graduating early ends them

The order of operations, year by year

1
Start the conversation early (14-15)Ask the team to add transition goals to the next IEP even if your state's floor is 16. Begin age-appropriate transition assessments - simple interest inventories, daily-living checklists, and observations - that drive the goals.
2
Write measurable postsecondary goalsFor each area (education/training, employment, and independent living if appropriate), the IEP states where your child is headed after school. Then annual goals and services are built to move toward them.
3
Invite the right peopleThe student is invited to their own meeting. With your consent, invite outside agencies likely to pay for or provide adult services - Vocational Rehabilitation, the county intellectual disability or waiver office, and Pre-ETS providers.
4
Connect to Pre-ETS and VR (around 15-16)Ask about Pre-Employment Transition Services through your state VR agency - job exploration, work-based learning, and self-advocacy training that can start while your child is still in school.
5
Handle the age of majority (before 18)Decide, with real information, between guardianship, a power of attorney, or supported decision-making. Do this months ahead - not the week of the birthday.
6
Exit with a Summary of PerformanceWhen your child leaves special education by graduating with a regular diploma or aging out, the school must provide a written Summary of Performance describing skills and recommendations to carry into adult life.

What transition planning actually is

Somewhere around your child's early teens, the IEP is supposed to grow a second job. Up to now it has answered "what does my child need this year?" Transition planning adds a bigger question: "what does adult life look like, and how do we get there?" It is a required, results-oriented part of the IEP under the federal special-education law (IDEA), and it is one of the most powerful tools you have - if the team treats it as more than a form.

The heart of it is a set of measurable postsecondary goals: short statements of where your child is headed after high school in education or training, in employment, and - when it fits - in independent living. Everything downstream (annual goals, services, coursework, work experiences) is supposed to point toward those goals. Done well, it is a map. Done as a checkbox, it is a page nobody reads.

A note that saves heartache: goals should reflect your real child, presuming competence, not a watered-down version. Nonverbal, medically complex, or significant-support kids still get transition goals - they may center on supported employment, community participation, communication, and daily living rather than a four-year college. Different destination, same right to a real plan.

The three (sometimes four) goal areas, explained

Education or training asks: after high school, will your child continue learning, and how? That could mean college with disability supports, a certificate program, a community-based transition program run by the school district until age 21, adult day services with a learning component, or on-the-job training. Employment asks what work looks like - competitive employment, supported or customized employment with a job coach, a sheltered or group setting, or volunteer roles that build skills.

Independent living goals are required when the team decides they are appropriate, and for kids with significant support needs they usually are. This is the real-life stuff: communication, self-care, mobility, using an AAC device in the community, managing a schedule, cooking, riding transit, staying safe. For families in the medically complex world, independent living goals are where the IEP meets the rest of your life - and where the plan can quietly fund practice that therapies alone never reach.

You will sometimes hear people treat these as boxes to fill. Push for goals that are specific and honest. "Teagan will use his eye-gaze device to make a purchase choice at a community store with one adult prompt" tells the team what to teach. "Teagan will improve independence" tells them nothing.

Pre-ETS and Vocational Rehabilitation: the agencies that pay

Two acronyms are worth learning early. Vocational Rehabilitation (VR) is your state agency that helps people with disabilities prepare for, get, and keep employment. Pre-Employment Transition Services (Pre-ETS) is a specific set of services - job exploration counseling, work-based learning, help understanding college or training options, workplace readiness, and self-advocacy instruction - that VR can provide to eligible or potentially eligible students while they are still in high school.

This matters because the school district and VR are two different funding streams, and the smoothest transitions braid them together. Ask the district's transition coordinator how to connect with VR in your state, and whether a Pre-ETS provider can begin working with your child now rather than at graduation. Waitlists and "order of selection" rules exist in some states, so getting on VR's radar early is a real advantage.

For kids who will need Medicaid-funded adult services - waiver programs, supported employment, adult day, residential supports - the other agency to invite (with your written consent) is your county or state intellectual disability / developmental disability office. Adult waiver waitlists can be long, and applications often need to start well before graduation. Getting that referral into the IEP conversation is one of the highest-leverage things you can do.

Age of majority: the birthday that changes who signs

In most states, when your child turns 18 they legally become an adult, and the educational rights you have been exercising - consenting to evaluations, agreeing to the IEP, requesting meetings - transfer to them. The school must notify you both before this happens. For a young person who can direct their own education, this is a milestone worth celebrating and preparing for with self-advocacy practice.

For a young person who cannot make or communicate complex decisions, this is something to plan for months in advance. Your options exist on a spectrum. Supported decision-making lets your adult child keep their rights while designating trusted people to help them understand and decide - the least restrictive option, increasingly recognized in law. A power of attorney lets them delegate specific decisions voluntarily. Guardianship is a court process that transfers decision-making authority and is the most restrictive - courts increasingly expect families to consider less restrictive alternatives first.

There is no single right answer, and the right choice is deeply individual and legally state-specific. What you do not want is to be blindsided the week of the birthday. Talk to a special-needs attorney or your state's protection and advocacy agency well ahead of time, and remember: this decision interacts with benefits, finances, and estate planning, so it rarely stands alone.

Graduation, aging out, and the Summary of Performance

Here is a trap worth flagging in bold: graduating with a regular high-school diploma generally ends your child's entitlement to IDEA special-education services. If your child still needs the school-based transition program, therapies, or community instruction that the district provides until the state's exit age (21 in many states, 22 in some), talk carefully with the team before agreeing to a diploma track versus a certificate of completion. This is a strategic decision, not a formality.

When your child does exit - by earning a regular diploma or by aging out - the district must provide a Summary of Performance (SOP). This is a written document describing your child's academic and functional performance and giving recommendations to help them meet their postsecondary goals. It becomes evidence in the adult world: colleges, VR, and disability offices often want it to document the need for accommodations, so make sure it is substantive, not a single vague page.

Aging out is emotional. The steady structure of school ends, and adult services can feel like a patchwork you assemble yourself. The families who land softest are the ones who started the adult-agency paperwork years early and kept a binder of evaluations, the SOP, and benefit letters ready to hand over.

How to prepare so the meeting works for you

Come to the IEP with a one-page vision of adult life in your child's own imagined voice - where they might live, what a good day looks like, what work or activity gives them purpose. It reorients the room away from deficits and toward a person. Bring your child if at all possible; even a short appearance, or a recorded message, or a slideshow through their AAC device, changes the tone and honors that this is their meeting.

Ask for the transition assessments in advance and read them. Ask how each annual goal connects to a postsecondary goal - if the team cannot answer, the plan is not aligned yet. Request that outside agencies be invited with your consent, and get the transition coordinator's direct contact. And keep everything: assessments, meeting notes, the SOP, agency referrals. In the adult system, your binder is often the only continuous record that follows your child.

Don't let an early diploma end services by accident

A regular diploma usually terminates IDEA eligibility - including the school-based transition program and therapies your child may still need until age 21 or 22. Before you agree to a graduation date or diploma track, ask the team exactly what services stop and what your child would lose. If they still need school-based supports, a certificate of completion may keep the door open. Decide this on purpose, not by default.

Frequently asked questions

When does transition planning legally have to start?
Under federal law (IDEA), transition planning must be in the IEP in effect when your child turns 16. Many states set an earlier floor - Pennsylvania, for example, requires it by 14. You can always ask the team to start sooner; nothing stops earlier planning, and earlier is almost always better.
My child is nonverbal and medically complex. Is transition planning even relevant?
Yes, completely. Transition goals still apply - they simply center on supported employment or community participation, communication, daily living, and independence at whatever level fits your child. Presume competence and insist on real, specific goals. A different destination is still a destination worth mapping.
Does turning 18 mean I lose all say in my child's education?
In most states, educational rights transfer to your child at 18, and the school must notify you both beforehand. Whether you keep a formal role depends on the path you plan for - supported decision-making, power of attorney, or guardianship. Talk to a special-needs attorney or your state's protection and advocacy agency well before the birthday, because the right choice is individual and state-specific.
What is Pre-ETS and how do we get it?
Pre-Employment Transition Services are job-focused services - exploration, work-based learning, workplace readiness, and self-advocacy - that your state Vocational Rehabilitation agency can provide while your child is still in school. Ask your district's transition coordinator how to connect with VR, and ask about starting now rather than at graduation, since some states have waitlists.
The school wants my child to graduate. Should I agree?
Not automatically. A regular diploma generally ends IDEA services, including the transition program that may run until 21 or 22 in your state. Ask precisely what stops and whether your child still needs those supports. If they do, a certificate of completion may preserve eligibility. Make it a deliberate decision.
What is a Summary of Performance and why does it matter?
When your child exits special education by graduating or aging out, the district must give you a written Summary of Performance describing their skills and offering recommendations for postsecondary goals. Colleges, VR, and disability offices often use it to document the need for accommodations, so push for a detailed one - it's a key that opens adult-world doors.
