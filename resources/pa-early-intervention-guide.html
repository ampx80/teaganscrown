How to start Early Intervention in PA

1
1. Make the referralAnyone can refer, including you. For ages 0-3 call the CONNECT Helpline at 1-800-692-7288. For ages 3-5 contact your county or local Preschool EI/intermediate unit. You do not need a doctor's note.
2
2. Get the free evaluationA team evaluates your child across areas like motor, communication, feeding, hearing, vision, and social-emotional skills. Ask for the evaluation in writing and share any medical records or diagnoses you have.
3
3. Build the IFSP or IEPIf eligible, you and the team write a plan. Ages 0-3 get an IFSP (Individualized Family Service Plan); ages 3-5 get an IEP (Individualized Education Program). You set goals that matter to your family.
4
4. Services beginTherapies (PT, OT, speech, developmental, vision, hearing) start, often in your home or child care for the youngest kids. A service coordinator helps you navigate.
5
5. Review and transitionPlans are reviewed regularly. Around your child's third birthday, the team plans the transition from Infant/Toddler EI to Preschool EI, and later to school-age services.

What Early Intervention actually is

Early Intervention (EI) is a set of free, publicly funded supports for very young children whose development is delayed or who have a diagnosed condition. The idea is simple and powerful: the brain grows fastest in the first years of life, so a little skilled help early can change a child's whole trajectory. In Pennsylvania, EI is run by the Office of Child Development and Early Learning (OCDEL).

EI is not a preschool and it is not babysitting. It is a team of professionals (physical, occupational, speech, developmental, vision, and hearing specialists) coaching you and working directly with your child on the everyday skills of moving, communicating, eating, playing, and connecting. For the youngest kids, much of it happens right in your living room during real daily routines.

Two programs, one system: 0-3 vs 3-5

Pennsylvania splits EI into two age bands, and knowing which one you are in saves confusion. The Infant/Toddler Early Intervention program serves birth to age 3 and is family-centered: the plan supports the whole family, and services usually come to you. The Preschool Early Intervention program serves ages 3 to 5 (until the child ages into kindergarten) and looks more like school-based special education, often delivered through your intermediate unit.

The eligibility rules and paperwork differ slightly between the two, and the transition around the third birthday is a formal handoff, not automatic sameness. If your child is already in the 0-3 program, ask your service coordinator to start transition planning several months before they turn 3 so there is no gap.

Who qualifies (and why you should refer even if you are unsure)

A child can qualify two main ways: by showing a measurable developmental delay, or by having a diagnosed physical or mental condition with a high probability of delay (for example cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, a significant hearing or vision loss, or a genetic condition). The exact delay percentage that counts varies by program and can change, so do not try to pre-judge the numbers yourself.

Here is the important part: there is no income limit, and referral is free. You lose nothing by asking for an evaluation, and you cannot 'use up' a benefit. Parents who wait to see if a child 'catches up' often lose months of the most valuable window. If something feels off, or a doctor, nurse, or child care provider raised a concern, refer now. The worst case is a reassuring 'your child is on track.'

IFSP vs IEP: your child's plan

For ages 0-3, the plan is an IFSP, an Individualized Family Service Plan. It is built around your family's routines and priorities and includes outcomes you help write, the services and how often they happen, and who coordinates everything. Because it is family-centered, the IFSP can address things like helping you feel confident feeding or positioning your child.

For ages 3-5, the plan becomes an IEP, an Individualized Education Program, focused on your child's learning and school-readiness goals. Either way, you are a full member of the team. You can request meetings, disagree, bring someone with you, and ask for changes. Keep a binder or folder of every evaluation, plan, and note; it becomes gold when you transition to school-age services or apply for other supports.

What it costs and how it connects to insurance and Medicaid

The EI evaluation and the services in your child's plan are provided at no cost to families in Pennsylvania. You do not pay out of pocket for the therapies written into the IFSP or IEP. With your permission, the program may bill your private insurance or Medicaid, but this is not supposed to reduce your benefits or cost you money for EI services.

EI also opens doors to the broader safety net. A child with significant needs may qualify for Medicaid regardless of family income through Pennsylvania's disability-based pathways (sometimes called the 'PH-95' or loophole category), which is separate from EI but often discovered through it. Your service coordinator can point you toward Medicaid, waiver programs, and other resources. If you hit a therapy limit or an insurance denial down the road, that is a signal to explore these medical assistance pathways, not to give up.

How to be a strong (not difficult) partner

The families who get the most from EI treat it as a team sport. Write down your concerns before evaluations. Say plainly what a normal day looks like and what you most want to change, whether that is your child taking a first supported step, tolerating food, or having a way to say 'more.' Ask the therapists to teach you what to practice between visits; carryover in daily routines is where the real gains happen.

You have rights, including written notice, informed consent, access to records, and a formal dispute process if you disagree. You rarely need to invoke them, but knowing they exist helps you speak up calmly. If you ever feel dismissed, ask for your concern and their answer to be written into the record. Documentation turns a stressful moment into a paper trail that protects your child.

Put your referral in writing and note the date

Federal rules give the 0-3 program a 45-day clock from referral to evaluation and first IFSP. If you call the CONNECT Helpline, follow up with an email or note the date, time, and who you spoke to. A written referral starts the timeline and gives you a record if things stall. Timelines and exact rules can vary, so confirm specifics with your local program.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a doctor's referral or diagnosis to start EI?
No. Anyone can refer, including you as the parent, and you do not need a diagnosis or a doctor's note. A concern is enough. If you do have medical records or a diagnosis, bring them, because they can speed up eligibility, but they are not required to begin.
Is Early Intervention really free even with our income?
Yes. Pennsylvania EI has no income limit, and the evaluation and services in your child's plan cost families nothing. With your consent the program may bill insurance or Medicaid, but that should not cost you money or reduce your family's benefits for EI services.
My child was born early or has a medical diagnosis. Do we still need a delay to qualify?
Often not. Children with certain diagnosed conditions that carry a high probability of delay can qualify based on the diagnosis itself, without waiting to measure a delay. Refer right away and share the diagnosis so the team can apply the correct pathway.
What happens when my child turns 3?
There is a formal transition from the Infant/Toddler (0-3) program to Preschool EI (3-5). It is not automatic sameness, so ask your service coordinator to start transition planning several months ahead. The goal is no gap in services and a smooth handoff of your child's information and goals.
How fast will services actually start?
For ages 0-3, federal rules set a 45-day timeline from referral to evaluation and the first IFSP meeting, with services following soon after. Timelines for the 3-5 program differ. If you feel things are moving too slowly, ask your service coordinator for the timeline in writing.
What if I disagree with the evaluation or the plan?
You are a full member of the team and have the right to disagree, request another meeting, and access a formal dispute process. Start by asking for your concern and the team's response to be written into the record. Most disagreements are resolved by talking, but the protections exist if you need them.
