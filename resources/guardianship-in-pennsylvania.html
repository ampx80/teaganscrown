Guardianship for a Child With Disabilities in Pennsylvania

At 18 your child becomes a legal adult overnight - here is how Pennsylvania guardianship works, the gentler alternatives to consider first, and exactly where to start.

What actually changes the day your child turns 18

In Pennsylvania, 18 is the age of majority. On that birthday your son or daughter becomes their own legal decision-maker - for medical care, money, contracts, education records, and where they live - no matter their diagnosis or communication ability. Doctors can stop talking to you. Schools can restrict records under FERPA. Banks answer to your child, not to you.

Guardianship is one legal tool that lets a parent or other trusted person keep making some or all of those decisions. It is powerful, and because it removes rights from an adult, Pennsylvania courts treat it seriously and increasingly ask whether something less sweeping would do the job.

Guardian of the person vs. of the estate - and plenary vs. limited

Two questions shape every PA guardianship. First, of what? A guardian of the person handles medical care, living arrangements, and daily welfare. A guardian of the estate handles money and property. A child with few assets may need a guardian of the person only; the court can appoint one, the other, or both.

Second, how much? A plenary (full) guardianship transfers essentially all decision-making. A limited guardianship transfers only the specific powers the court names and lets your child keep the rest - say, choosing where to volunteer while a parent handles medical consent. PA law favors the least restrictive arrangement that keeps your child safe, so limited guardianship is worth asking for when it fits.

The gentler alternatives to try first

Guardianship is not the only path, and for many families it is more than they need. Supported decision-making lets your child keep their rights while a team of trusted people helps them understand choices and communicate them. A financial or health care power of attorney works if your young adult can understand and sign one - it delegates authority without going to court. A health care representative under PA law can make medical decisions when no agent is named.

For benefits specifically, becoming a Social Security representative payee lets you manage your child's SSI without any guardianship at all. Signing HIPAA and FERPA releases keeps doctors and schools talking to you. Many families combine a few of these tools and never file for guardianship - or file only a narrow, limited one.

How the Pennsylvania process actually works

Guardianship of an adult runs through the Orphans' Court division of the Court of Common Pleas in the county where your child lives. You file a petition alleging incapacity and asking to be appointed. The court needs evidence - typically a report or live testimony from a physician, psychologist, or other qualified professional explaining your child's functional abilities and needs, not just a label.

Your child (the law calls them the alleged incapacitated person) has real due-process rights: notice of the hearing, the right to be present, the right to counsel, and the right to object. Recent Pennsylvania reforms strengthened these protections - courts give closer attention to whether counsel is appointed and whether less-restrictive alternatives were genuinely considered. A hearing follows, and if the court finds incapacity it issues a decree and letters of guardianship. Many uncontested cases are relatively straightforward, but the timeline and paperwork are why families start months ahead.

What guardianship costs - and how to get help

Costs vary by county and by whether you hire an attorney. Expect a county filing fee plus, in most cases, attorney fees; some evaluations carry a cost too. Exact amounts differ across Pennsylvania's 67 counties, so call your county Orphans' Court clerk and ask about current filing fees before you budget.

You do not have to do this alone or at full price. Legal aid organizations, law school clinics, your county's disability or aging services, the ARC of Pennsylvania chapters, and Disability Rights Pennsylvania can point you to low-cost or free help. Ask your child's transition coordinator or Supports Coordinator too - guardianship questions come up every year at IEP transition meetings.

Guardianship is not a money tool - protect benefits separately

An important myth to retire: guardianship does not create income, pay for care, or protect SSI and Medicaid. It only assigns decision-making. In fact, if a guardian of the estate receives money on your child's behalf, holding it the wrong way can jeopardize needs-based benefits.

Keep the legal and the financial planning on separate tracks. Tools like an ABLE account and a special needs trust are how families protect assets without losing SSI or a Medicaid waiver. Pair your guardianship decision with real benefits and estate planning so one does not accidentally undo the other.

After you are appointed: the ongoing duties

Guardianship is a role, not a one-time filing. A guardian of the person generally files periodic reports with the court on your child's wellbeing and living situation. A guardian of the estate must file an inventory of assets and regular accountings showing how money was managed.

Keep organized records, act only in your child's best interest, and use the least restrictive approach as their abilities grow. Courts can modify or end a guardianship if circumstances change - guardianship can become more limited over time, not just less.

Start before the birthday, not after

The single most common regret parents share is waiting until 18 to begin. Evaluations, notice, and hearings take weeks to months, and there can be a gap where you have no legal authority. Ask about guardianship at the IEP transition meeting and file the petition well before the 18th birthday so authority is in place when you need it. And ask the court for limited guardianship where it fits - you can always expand it, and PA law prefers the least restrictive option.

Frequently asked questions

Do I automatically become my child's guardian because I am the parent?
No. Parental authority ends at 18 in Pennsylvania. To keep making decisions you either use an alternative like a power of attorney or representative payee, or you petition Orphans' Court to be appointed guardian. Being the parent helps your case but does not make it automatic.
Can my child object to guardianship?
Yes. Your child has the right to notice, to attend the hearing, to be represented by counsel, and to object. Pennsylvania courts take these due-process rights seriously and look closely at whether a less-restrictive option would work. Many cases are still uncontested, but the rights are real.
Will guardianship affect my child's SSI or Medicaid waiver?
Guardianship itself does not create income or protect benefits - it only assigns decision-making. But how a guardian of the estate holds money can affect needs-based benefits. Keep benefits protected separately using tools like an ABLE account or special needs trust, and coordinate the two.
How long does the process take in Pennsylvania?
It varies by county and by whether the case is contested, but plan for weeks to a few months once you factor in the evaluation, filing, notice period, and hearing. That is why families are urged to start roughly six months before the 18th birthday.
Do I need a lawyer to file for guardianship?
Not strictly, but guardianship removes an adult's rights and the paperwork and evidence rules are exacting, so many families use an attorney. If cost is a barrier, legal aid, law school clinics, ARC chapters, and Disability Rights Pennsylvania can help you find low-cost or free assistance.
Can a guardianship be changed later?
Yes. Courts can modify or terminate a guardianship if your child's abilities or circumstances change. A guardianship can become more limited over time as your young adult grows, not only more restrictive.
