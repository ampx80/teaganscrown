The one rule that changes everything: itemizing

Here is the honest truth most articles bury: the medical expense deduction only works if you itemize on Schedule A instead of taking the standard deduction. Because the standard deduction is large, plenty of families never clear it - and that is not failure, it is just math. So before you spend a weekend sorting receipts, ask the bigger question: will your medical costs, plus state and local taxes, mortgage interest, and charitable giving, add up to more than the standard deduction for your filing status?

For families with a child who has significant medical needs, the answer is often yes - especially in a heavy year with surgery, new equipment, or a special-education placement. Spinal-fusion surgery, a wheelchair-accessible van, a stander, an eye-gaze device, months of therapy: these are exactly the kinds of costs that push a family over the line. And even if you do not clear it this year, keep reading - several of the best breaks below do not require itemizing at all.

What actually counts (the list is bigger than you think)

IRS Publication 502 is the master list, and it is more generous than most parents realize. Beyond the obvious doctor and hospital bills, you can generally include: prescription medications, therapy (PT, OT, speech, feeding, and more), medical equipment like standers, gait trainers, wheelchairs, AAC and eye-gaze devices, orthotics and AFOs, hearing aids, glasses, tube-feeding supplies, incontinence supplies prescribed for a condition, and the cost of a service animal including its food and vet care.

It also reaches into places people miss: nursing-type care in your home (even when provided by someone who is not a licensed nurse, if the services are medical in nature), the extra cost of special food or formula over ordinary food when prescribed, and premiums you pay out of pocket for medical and dental insurance. The rule of thumb: if a cost is there primarily to diagnose, treat, or ease your child's medical condition, it is probably in the club. When it is a gray area, a doctor's note is what settles the argument.

Home modifications and equipment: watch the fair-market-value catch

Making your home work for your child - a ramp, a roll-in shower, a ceiling lift, widened doorways, grab bars, a stair lift - can be deductible as a medical capital expense. But there is a twist worth understanding. A capital improvement is only deductible to the extent its cost exceeds the increase it adds to your home's fair market value. If a $12,000 elevator raises your home's value by $8,000, only $4,000 counts.

The good news: many accessibility features are presumed not to raise a home's value at all, which means the full cost is deductible. The IRS specifically points to things like building entrance ramps, widening doorways and hallways, installing grab bars and railings, lowering kitchen cabinets, and modifying outlets, fixtures, and bathrooms for accessibility. Ongoing operation and upkeep of medical equipment counts too. Keep the contractor's itemized invoice, and get a physician's letter stating the modification is medically necessary for your child.

Special school, therapies, and tutoring

This is one of the most valuable - and most misunderstood - deductions for our families. Tuition, meals, and lodging at a school that furnishes special education to help a child overcome a disability can be a deductible medical expense, but only when overcoming the disability is the primary reason the child attends. Any regular academic education must be incidental to the special services. A mainstream private school chosen for smaller classes does not qualify; a program designed around your child's medical or developmental condition may.

The door is open wider than tuition, too. Fees you pay - on a doctor's recommendation - for tutoring by a teacher specially trained to work with children who have learning disabilities caused by a mental or physical impairment can also count. So can the medically necessary portion of a therapeutic program. Because these claims draw scrutiny, the physician's recommendation and the school's description of its special-education services are the documents that carry the day.

Mileage, travel, and medical conferences

All those drives add up, and the IRS lets you count them. For 2025 you can deduct 21 cents per mile driven primarily for medical care, plus parking and tolls, instead of tracking actual gas costs. Log the therapy runs, specialist appointments, hospital trips, and pharmacy pickups - a simple note in your phone with date, destination, and miles is enough. Rates change each year, so confirm the current figure when you file.

Travel for care can qualify beyond mileage. Transportation costs to see a specialist in another city can count, and lodging may be included up to a modest per-night limit when the trip is essentially for medical care and there is no significant element of vacation. There is also a lesser-known one: the cost of admission and transportation to a medical conference about your child's chronic condition can be deductible - though meals and lodging while attending that conference are not. Keep the agenda and proof that the sessions related to your child's specific diagnosis.

Beyond the deduction: ABLE accounts, FSAs, and credits

If you take the standard deduction, you are not shut out - some of the biggest wins live outside Schedule A. An ABLE account lets a person whose disability began before age 26 (this age threshold rises to 46 for tax years starting in 2026) save money that grows tax-free and can be spent on a broad list of disability expenses, all without jeopardizing SSI and Medicaid up to program limits. The 2025 contribution cap is $19,000, with an extra allowance for beneficiaries who work. Lower-income beneficiaries may even claim the Saver's Credit on their contributions using Form 8880.

Two more that ignore the itemizing question entirely: a Dependent Care FSA through your employer lets you set aside pre-tax dollars for care so you can work - and it can cover a disabled dependent of any age, not just young children (the annual limit was recently raised, so check the current figure). And the Child and Dependent Care Credit works the same way, covering care for a dependent of any age who is physically or mentally incapable of self-care. You generally cannot use the same dollars for both, but a tax pro can help you split them to squeeze out maximum benefit.

The paperwork that protects you

Deductions are won and lost on documentation. Build one folder (paper or digital) per tax year and drop in: EOBs and receipts showing what you actually paid, pharmacy year-end printouts, your mileage log, itemized invoices for any equipment or home mods, and - the crown jewel - letters of medical necessity from your child's physicians for anything the IRS might question. A one-paragraph letter tying a ramp, a special diet, a device, or a school to a diagnosed condition transforms a shaky claim into a solid one.

One rule to tattoo on your brain: you can only deduct what you were not reimbursed for. Expenses paid by insurance, or with pre-tax HSA or FSA dollars, cannot be double-counted on Schedule A. Track reimbursements as carefully as you track costs, and when a year is unusually expensive, that is precisely when it pays to sit down with a preparer who knows special-needs tax rules cold.

Run the numbers both ways before you assume you get nothing

The single most expensive mistake is skipping the medical deduction because 'we never itemize,' or skipping ABLE and the care credit because 'we don't make enough to matter.' A heavy medical year can flip the itemizing math overnight, and the ABLE, FSA, and credit paths help even families who take the standard deduction. And remember: you can never deduct a cost that insurance, an HSA, or an FSA already reimbursed - double-dipping is exactly what triggers trouble.

Frequently asked questions

We don't have enough deductions to itemize. Is there anything for us?
Yes - and this is the most important thing to hear. ABLE accounts, a Dependent Care FSA, and the Child and Dependent Care Credit all work whether or not you itemize. Many families who 'get nothing' from Schedule A save real money through these instead. Do not stop at the medical deduction.
My child is over 18. Can I still claim these deductions?
In most cases, yes. When an adult child cannot support themselves because of a disability and remains your dependent, their medical costs are deductible on your return, and care costs can qualify for the Child and Dependent Care Credit at any age. The disability rules were written with exactly this situation in mind.
Do I really need a doctor's note for everything?
Not for obvious medical bills like a hospital or pharmacy receipt. But for anything the IRS might question - a home ramp, a special school, a special diet, a service animal, tutoring - a letter of medical necessity tying the cost to your child's diagnosis is what protects the deduction. When in doubt, get the letter.
Can I deduct my child's private special-education school tuition?
Possibly, but only when the primary reason your child attends is to receive special education for their disability, with ordinary academics being incidental. A mainstream private school chosen for smaller class sizes does not qualify. Keep the school's description of its special-education program and your doctor's recommendation.
Can I count what I pay a family member or aide to care for my child?
Payments for nursing-type or medical care can be deductible as a medical expense, even if the caregiver is not a licensed nurse, as long as the services are genuinely medical in nature. Care that lets you work may instead qualify for the Dependent Care FSA or credit. Keep records of who you paid, how much, and for what.
This is overwhelming. Is a tax preparer worth it?
For a family with significant medical and disability costs, very often yes. Special-needs returns touch deductions, ABLE, FSAs, credits, and dependency rules that trip up general software. A preparer who knows this territory frequently saves far more than their fee - and spares you the stress in an already-full life.
