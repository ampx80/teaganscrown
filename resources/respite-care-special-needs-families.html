Finding and Funding Respite Care

Respite care is not a luxury or a sign you are failing - it is oxygen for the person holding everyone else up, and there are real ways to pay for it.

What respite care actually is (and is not)

Respite care is short-term, temporary care that gives a family caregiver a break - a few hours to shower and sleep, an afternoon for a sibling's game, or a weekend to breathe. The care can happen in your home, at a provider's home, at a day program, or through a specialized camp or crisis nursery. It is planned or emergency, occasional or regular. There is no single right amount.

It is not giving up, and it is not the same as long-term or residential placement. Think of it as maintenance for the caregiver so the whole family can keep going. Airlines tell you to secure your own oxygen mask first for a reason - respite is that mask.

Why respite is a medical necessity, not a treat

Parents of children with complex needs often run on interrupted sleep, hypervigilance, and years without a real day off. That is a recipe for caregiver burnout, depression, injury from lifting and transfers, and marriages and sibling relationships stretched thin. Rested caregivers make safer decisions, catch medical changes sooner, and simply last longer.

Framing respite as care for the caregiver matters when you ask for it. On waiver plans and in nonprofit applications, respite is treated as a legitimate, fundable service - not an indulgence. You are allowed to need it.

Start with Medicaid: EPSDT and HCBS waivers

If your child has Medicaid, start there. Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers - the disability or medically-fragile waivers that vary by state - very commonly list respite as a covered service. Once your child is enrolled, your case manager or support coordinator can add respite hours to the service plan, and Medicaid pays the provider directly.

EPSDT (Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment) is Medicaid's benefit for kids under 21 and requires coverage of medically necessary services. EPSDT is the engine behind a lot of pediatric coverage, though respite specifically usually flows through a waiver rather than the standard benefit. The catch: many waivers have waitlists that can run years, so the single most important move is to get your child on the waiver waitlist as early as possible, even if you do not need services yet.

Lifespan Respite, vouchers, and caregiver programs

No waiver? You still have options. The federal Lifespan Respite Care Act funds a program in most states designed to help families of any age or diagnosis find and pay for respite - often through vouchers or reimbursement that you can use with a provider of your choice. Search 'Lifespan Respite' plus your state to find yours.

Your Area Agency on Aging and state disability agency may also run Family Caregiver Support Programs with small respite grants. These programs are usually not means-tested the way Medicaid is, and the application is often lighter - a real advantage when you need a break sooner rather than later.

Where to actually find a provider you trust

The ARCH National Respite Network and Resource Center runs a free National Respite Locator that lets you search for respite services by state and zip. Start there, then work your network: your child's case manager, your local Parent to Parent chapter, The Arc, Easterseals, and UCP affiliates often keep names of trained, vetted providers.

Do not overlook people-powered respite. Some churches and community groups host respite nights where volunteers care for children with disabilities so parents get an evening off. Special-needs camps and day programs double as multi-day respite. And a trained family member, a college student in a related major, or a former therapy aide can become a paid provider under many voucher and self-directed waiver programs.

Paying out of pocket - and stretching every dollar

Sometimes you need care before any program comes through. When paying privately, ask providers about sliding-scale rates, share a provider's hours with another special-needs family, and use flexible dollars you may already have - an ABLE account can hold savings for disability expenses without risking benefits, and some Special Needs Trusts can pay for respite.

Crowdfunding can fund a specific, time-limited respite need - for example, covering coverage during a parent's own surgery or recovery. Be concrete about what the money buys, and pair it with the free and waiver options above so you are not relying on donations for an ongoing need.

Vetting a respite provider so you can truly relax

A break only helps if you are not anxious the whole time. Before a first solo shift, do a paid trial visit while you are home. Walk through your child's routine, medications, feeding, seizure or breathing protocols, communication device or system, and what a good day and a hard day look like. Write a one-page care sheet with emergency contacts and post it on the fridge.

Ask about training, CPR and first aid, experience with your child's specific equipment, and references - and trust your gut. Agencies typically run background checks; if you hire privately, run one yourself. The goal is a provider who is competent and calm, so the person who never gets a break finally can.

Get on the waiver waitlist today - even if you do not need respite yet

Medicaid HCBS waiver waitlists in many states run months to years. Your child's spot is often based on the date you apply, not the date you need help. Call your state's developmental disability or Medicaid office and ask to be added now. It costs nothing, and future-you may get respite years sooner because present-you made one phone call.

Frequently asked questions

Does insurance or Medicaid pay for respite care?
Private insurance usually does not cover respite, but Medicaid often does through an HCBS waiver, where respite is a common covered service and the provider is paid directly. Coverage, waitlists, and hour limits vary by state and by waiver, so ask your case manager or state Medicaid office exactly what your plan includes.
We do not have a Medicaid waiver. Can we still get help paying for respite?
Yes. Every state has a Lifespan Respite program or Family Caregiver Support Program that may offer vouchers or reimbursement regardless of diagnosis, and these usually have lighter applications than Medicaid. Local nonprofits like Easterseals, The Arc, and UCP affiliates, plus church and community respite nights, can also provide free or low-cost breaks.
How do I find a respite provider I can actually trust with my child?
Start with the free ARCH National Respite Locator, then ask your case manager, Parent to Parent chapter, and local disability nonprofits for vetted names. Before a solo shift, do a paid trial visit while you are home, write a one-page care sheet, confirm training and CPR, and run or confirm a background check.
Can a family member or friend be paid to provide respite?
Often, yes. Many voucher programs and self-directed Medicaid waivers let you hire and pay someone you choose - sometimes including certain relatives, a trusted student, or a former therapy aide - as long as they meet the program's requirements. Ask your support coordinator or Lifespan Respite program which relatives qualify, since rules vary by state and program.
How much respite care can we get?
There is no universal amount. Waivers set hour limits that differ by state and by a child's assessed needs; voucher and nonprofit programs may cap dollars or events per year. The amount can also change as your child's needs change, so revisit it at each plan review and ask for more when your situation shifts.
Is it selfish to want a break from caring for my child?
No. Respite is care for the caregiver, and rested parents are safer, more present, and able to keep going for the long haul. Waiver plans and nonprofits treat respite as a legitimate, fundable service precisely because the caregiver's health protects the whole family. Needing a break is human, not selfish.
