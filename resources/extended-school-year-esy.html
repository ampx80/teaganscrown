Extended School Year (ESY): Do You Qualify?

ESY is not summer school and it is not a reward for good grades - it is free special education over the break for kids who would otherwise lose hard-won skills, and the law is on your side.

$0

The team decides

What ESY Is (and What It Is Absolutely Not)

Extended School Year is special education and related services provided beyond the normal school calendar, at no cost to you, when your child's IEP team decides they need it to receive a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE). It is guaranteed under the federal special education law, IDEA.

Here is the mix-up that costs families dearly: ESY is not summer school, not tutoring, not enrichment, and not a standard 'six weeks of camp' that every kid with an IEP gets. It is individualized. Your child gets the specific goals and services they need to hold onto progress - which might be an hour of speech twice a week, or a full-day program, or physical therapy to keep a skill from slipping. It also is not automatically a copy of the school-year IEP; the team decides which goals are at risk and targets those.

The Real Question: Regression and Recoupment

The most common standard teams use is regression and recoupment. Regression means how much your child loses over a break. Recoupment means how long it takes to get that skill back once school resumes. If your child loses skills and cannot recover them in a reasonable time, that points toward ESY eligibility.

This is why data matters so much. If a therapist can show that after winter break your child needed six weeks to relearn how to use their AAC device to request items, that is powerful evidence. Ask the team, in writing and early, to collect baseline data before breaks and probe data after them. You can also keep your own simple notes at home - what your child could do in June versus what disappeared by August.

Beyond Regression: The Other Factors Teams Must Weigh

Regression is the headline, but courts and guidance have made clear it is not the only factor - and a school cannot deny ESY just because it has no perfect regression data. Teams are also supposed to consider emerging skills or 'breakthrough' opportunities: a skill your child is right on the edge of mastering, where a long break would waste the momentum.

Other legitimate factors include the severity of your child's disability, the nature and degree of their needs, whether the skill is 'critical' (like communication, mobility, self-feeding, or safety behaviors), the presence of interfering behaviors, and the child's ability to interact with nondisabled peers. For a child like Teagan - nonverbal, using an eye-gaze device and a gait trainer - communication and mobility skills are exactly the kind of critical, hard-won gains that a summer of nothing can quietly erode.

How to Ask - and What to Put in Writing

Start early. By late fall or winter, send a short email to your case manager: 'I would like ESY considered for my child. Please begin collecting regression and recoupment data around breaks, and place ESY on the agenda for our next IEP meeting.' Dated, written requests create a paper trail and often move things faster than a hallway conversation.

At the meeting, bring your own observations, therapy notes, private evaluations, and specific examples of skills lost over past breaks. Ask that the team's ESY decision - and its reasoning - be written into the IEP and reflected in your Prior Written Notice (PWN). If they say no, the PWN must explain why; that document becomes your roadmap if you appeal.

If They Say No: Your Appeal Options

A denial is a decision you can challenge, not a verdict. Your options generally escalate: request another IEP meeting to present more data, file for state mediation (free and often quick), or file a due process complaint. You can also file a state complaint with your department of education if you believe procedures were violated.

Timing is everything with ESY because the need is seasonal - a hearing that resolves in the fall does you no good for the summer that already passed. If you are denied and disagree, act quickly and consider asking a special education attorney or your state's Parent Training and Information (PTI) center for help. Many PTI centers assist for free. Denials over sparse data are common and very much worth pushing back on.

Where ESY Fits in the Bigger Support Picture

ESY covers the education side of the summer, but it rarely covers everything a medically complex child needs when school is out. Many families layer ESY with Medicaid-funded services - a home-and-community-based services (HCBS) waiver may fund respite, personal care, or private therapies that fill gaps ESY does not touch. EPSDT, the child health benefit within Medicaid, can also cover medically necessary therapies year-round.

Think of it as a stack: ESY holds the educational and developmental line, waiver services and EPSDT handle care and medical therapy, and respite grants give the family breathing room. No single program does it all, and none of them will hand you the others - you assemble the stack yourself, and that is exactly the kind of navigation this guide exists to make easier.

Do not wait for spring

The single biggest mistake families make is raising ESY too late. Ask in the fall or winter that the team start collecting regression and recoupment data around breaks. If you wait until April, the school may say it has 'no data' to justify ESY - and that missing data becomes the reason for a denial. Get it in writing early.

Frequently asked questions

My child gets good grades. Does that automatically disqualify them from ESY?
No. ESY is not tied to grades. A child can do well academically and still qualify because of regression in critical skills like communication, mobility, behavior, or self-care. The question is whether skills are lost over breaks and are slow to recover - not report cards.
The school said my child 'doesn't qualify' before we even had a meeting. Is that allowed?
No. ESY eligibility must be an individualized IEP-team decision based on your child's data, not a blanket policy or a predetermined answer. A district cannot limit ESY to certain disability categories or refuse to consider it. If you hear a blanket 'no,' request a formal team decision and Prior Written Notice in writing.
How many hours or weeks of ESY will my child get?
There is no fixed number - it is individualized. ESY could be a few therapy sessions a week or a full-day program, depending on which goals are at risk. Be cautious of a district offering the exact same 'ESY package' to every child; that is a sign of a program, not an individualized IEP decision.
Does ESY have to happen in the summer?
Not necessarily. ESY means services beyond the standard school year, so it can also apply to long winter or spring breaks if that is when your child regresses. It is defined by need and timing, not by the word 'summer.'
We were denied ESY. What is the fastest thing we can do?
Request another IEP meeting right away and bring your own data, then consider free state mediation if that fails. Because ESY is seasonal, move quickly - a delayed appeal can outlast the break itself. Your state's Parent Training and Information center can often help at no cost.
Does ESY cover related services like PT, OT, and speech?
It can. ESY is not limited to academics - if the team finds your child needs a related service to prevent regression, that service can be part of ESY. For many medically complex kids, the related services (like mobility or communication therapy) are the whole point of ESY.
