Start with your child, not the brochure

Before you look at a single camp website, write down what your child actually needs to be safe and happy for a day or a week. Think in plain terms: Does someone need to manage seizures or medications? Is there a feeding tube, a special diet, or a swallowing concern? Does your child use a wheelchair, gait trainer, or AAC device? Does a loud, busy environment lead to meltdowns, or is that exactly the stimulation they crave?

This list is your filter. It turns an overwhelming search into a simple yes-or-no test for every camp: can they meet these needs, or not? It also becomes the script for your phone calls later. You are not being difficult by asking, you are being a good advocate, and the best camps expect these questions.

Specialized camp or inclusive camp?

There are two broad paths. A fully specialized camp is designed from the ground up for children with disabilities or a specific diagnosis, often with nurses on site, wheelchair-accessible everything, and staff trained in complex care. These can be a huge relief for kids with high medical or support needs.

An inclusive (or mainstream) camp welcomes children of all abilities together, sometimes with an aide or accommodations added in. This can be wonderful for a child who thrives alongside typically developing peers and siblings. Neither is better in the abstract, it depends entirely on your child. Some families rotate: a specialized medical camp one week, an inclusive day camp the next.

Where to actually find camps

Start with diagnosis-specific nonprofits, because they often run or vet camps for exactly your child's needs. Groups tied to cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, autism, spina bifida, epilepsy, and vision or hearing loss frequently list trusted camps and scholarships. Easterseals runs accessible camps and programs in many regions, and Variety - the Children's Charity supports camp experiences for kids with disabilities in some areas.

The American Camp Association (ACA) lets you filter accredited camps by special needs and medical support, which is a good sign a camp meets basic safety and staffing standards. Your state's Parent Training and Information Center, your Early Intervention or school district contacts, and local parent Facebook groups are gold too, because they surface camps that reviews and directories miss.

Also ask your child's therapists and specialists directly. PTs, OTs, speech therapists, and clinic social workers often know which local camps handle real medical complexity well, and which ones say yes on the phone but struggle in practice.

The questions that separate great camps from risky ones

When you call, go past the friendly overview. Ask: What is your staff-to-camper ratio, and can you provide 1:1 support? Is a nurse on site, and who is allowed to give medications or manage a tube feed? How are your counselors trained for seizures, behavior, or medical emergencies, and how far is the nearest hospital?

Then ask the question that reveals everything: 'Tell me about a time a camper had a hard day, and what your staff did.' A camp that answers with specifics and warmth is showing you their real culture. A camp that gets defensive or vague is telling you something too. Trust that.

Real ways to help pay for camp

Camp can be expensive, but there are more funding doors than most families realize. Start with Medicaid. Some Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers cover therapeutic day programs, respite, or camp-like services, though what counts varies a lot by state and by your specific waiver. Ask your waiver case manager or support coordinator, 'Can any of my child's authorized services be used toward a summer camp or day program?' You may be surprised.

Next, look at scholarships. Many specialized camps have their own camperships (need-based aid), and diagnosis-specific nonprofits and local groups like the Elks, Lions Club, Kiwanis, and community foundations often fund camp for a child with a disability. If your child gets SSI, that monthly benefit can help cover fees. An ABLE account lets you save for disability-related expenses, which can include camp, without risking benefit eligibility.

Finally, if the camp is genuinely part of your child's therapy or care plan, ask whether any portion could be medically justified through insurance or a waiver, and keep documentation from your child's providers. It does not always work, but it is worth the ask.

Timing: why early really matters

The best special-needs camps and the scholarship money that makes them possible tend to fill up months before summer, sometimes by late winter. Waiver approvals and service authorizations can also take weeks, so a January or February start gives you room to breathe.

If you have missed the early window, do not give up. Ask about waitlists, cancellations, and shorter day-camp options, and check whether a nearby camp added a session. Families drop spots for all kinds of reasons, and a warm, prepared call can move you up fast.

Set your child (and the camp) up to succeed

Once you choose a camp, over-communicate. Send a short one-page profile of your child: what calms them, what sets them off, how they communicate, their exact medical routine, and what a good day looks like. Label everything, pack extras of anything essential, and if your child uses an AAC device or specific equipment, make sure staff know how it works before day one.

Ask about a phone or video meet-and-greet with the counselor, and if possible a short visit to the space beforehand. A little familiarity turns a scary new place into a slightly-less-scary new place, which is often the difference between a great first day and a hard one.

Frequently asked questions

Will Medicaid or my child's waiver pay for summer camp?
Sometimes. Some Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers cover therapeutic day programs, respite, or camp-like services, but coverage varies a lot by state and by your specific waiver. The only way to know is to ask your case manager or support coordinator directly, ideally before you pay a deposit, and get any approval in writing.
How early should I start looking?
Earlier than feels necessary. Popular special-needs camps and their scholarship funds often fill by late winter, and waiver authorizations can take weeks. Starting in January or February gives you the best shot at both a spot and the funding to pay for it. If you're late, ask about waitlists and cancellations anyway.
My child has serious medical needs. Are there camps that can actually handle that?
Yes. Fully specialized camps often have nurses on site, staff trained in seizures, feeding, and medication management, and fully accessible facilities. Ask pointed questions: nurse coverage, who can give meds or manage a tube, staff-to-camper ratio, 1:1 support, and distance to the nearest hospital. Diagnosis-specific nonprofits and your child's therapists can point you to the ones that handle complexity well.
What's the difference between a specialized camp and an inclusive camp?
A specialized camp is built specifically for children with disabilities, often with medical staff and adaptive everything. An inclusive camp welcomes kids of all abilities together, sometimes with an added aide or accommodations. The right choice depends on your child, whether they need intensive support or thrive alongside typically developing peers.
How can I afford it if we're on a tight budget?
Stack your options. Many camps offer need-based scholarships (camperships). Civic groups like the Elks, Lions Club, and Kiwanis, plus diagnosis-specific nonprofits, often fund camp for a child with a disability. If your child receives SSI, that can help, and an ABLE account lets you save for disability-related costs like camp without risking benefits. Ask every single camp what aid they offer, because many don't advertise it.
How do I help my child adjust to a new camp?
Over-communicate with staff. Send a one-page profile covering how your child communicates, what calms and upsets them, and their exact medical routine. Ask for a phone or video meet-and-greet with the counselor and, if possible, a short visit beforehand. Familiarity turns a scary new place into a manageable one, and it helps staff succeed too.
