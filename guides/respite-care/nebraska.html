A practical parent's guide

Respite care for families raising a child with disabilities in Nebraska

What respite is, why rest is essential for caregivers, how it may be paid for, and the honest ways Nebraska families find help - explained in plain language for parents who are already stretched thin.

Apply for help Official resources

What respite care is

Respite care is short-term, temporary care for your child that gives you, the primary caregiver, a planned break. It might be a few hours on a Saturday, an afternoon each week, a full day, or an occasional overnight. The care can happen in your own home, in a provider's home, or in a community setting, depending on your child's needs and what a given program allows.

The idea is simple and human. Someone you trust steps in to care for your child so you can rest, sleep, run errands, work, keep a medical appointment of your own, or spend focused time with your other children or your partner. Respite is not about stepping away from your child. It is about making sure you can keep going.

Rest is not a reward you earn after the caregiving is done. For a parent raising a child with disabilities, rest is part of how the caregiving gets done at all.

Why respite matters for caregiver wellbeing

Raising a child with disabilities can be around-the-clock work. The physical lifting, the medical management, the appointments, the advocacy, and the constant vigilance add up, often with very few natural breaks. Over time, that unrelenting load can wear down even the strongest, most devoted parent.

Regular respite gives caregivers a chance to recover. Families and clinicians widely recognize that planned breaks can help protect a caregiver's physical health, ease stress and isolation, and reduce the risk of burnout. When you are rested, you are steadier, more patient, and better able to make good decisions for your child. Caring for yourself is not separate from caring for your child. It is part of the same job.

It also helps the whole family. Siblings get some undivided attention, relationships get room to breathe, and the household runs a little less on empty. None of that is selfish. It is how families sustain the long, loving work of raising a child who needs more.

How respite care may be funded

One of the most common questions families ask is whether respite has to come out of their own pocket. Often, it does not have to. Respite is a service that a number of funding pathways can help pay for, though what is available depends on your child's situation and is set at the state level.

  • Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers. Respite is one of the supports many HCBS waivers commonly cover. These waivers are authorized under federal law and run by each state, so the specific programs, covered hours, and rules are defined by Nebraska, not by a national standard.
  • State family-support or respite programs. Some states offer family-support, respite-subsidy, or voucher programs outside of Medicaid to help families with the cost of a break. Whether such help exists and who qualifies is decided at the state level.
  • Grants and nonprofits. Disability nonprofits and community organizations sometimes offer respite grants, scholarships, or free respite events, which can help while you wait for other funding or fill a gap it does not cover.

Because programs, eligibility rules, and covered hours change and are specific to each state, do not go by amounts or rules you read online. Confirm what your family can access by contacting your Nebraska developmental-disabilities agency, your Medicaid office, or a state respite coalition.

A note on waitlists

Some waiver and family-support programs serve a limited number of people at a time, so families are sometimes placed on an interest list or waiting list. If that happens, add your child's name as early as you can, keep your contact information current, and ask about grants, nonprofits, and free respite events that can help in the meantime. Being on a list costs nothing and keeps the door open.

How families find respite providers

Finding the right person or program can feel daunting, especially the first time. Most families get there through one or more of these paths:

  1. Through a service coordinator. If your child is enrolled in a Medicaid waiver, ask your service coordinator or case manager to connect you with approved respite providers and explain how the service works.
  2. Through your state disability agency. Nebraska's developmental-disabilities agency can point you toward respite options, family-support programs, and provider information.
  3. Through a respite coalition or registry. Many states have a respite coalition or a provider registry that helps families locate and match with respite workers in their area.
  4. Through your own circle. Extended family, experienced sitters, faith communities, and other special-needs parents are often part of a family's respite plan. Some programs even let you hire and direct a respite worker you already know and trust.
  5. Through parent centers and nonprofits. A local parent center can offer free, one-on-one guidance on your options, and disability nonprofits sometimes host respite events or maintain lists of vetted providers.

Whoever you choose, take time to prepare them well. Write down your child's routines, medications, communication style, likes and dislikes, and what to do in an emergency. A little preparation helps a new provider feel confident and helps you actually relax while you are away.

If the process feels slow or overwhelming, you are not doing anything wrong. The system has gaps, and asking for a break can feel hard when you are used to carrying everything. But you deserve rest, and reaching out for respite is a sign of strength, not weakness.

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

What is respite care?

Respite care is short-term, temporary care for your child that gives you, the primary caregiver, a planned break. It can happen in your home or in another setting, and it can last a few hours, a full day, or longer, depending on your family's needs and what a program allows.

The goal is simple: to let a caregiver rest and recover so they can keep going for the long haul.

Why does respite matter so much for caregivers?

Caring for a child with disabilities is often around-the-clock work, and exhaustion builds up in ways that affect your health, your relationships, and your ability to care well. Regular breaks are not a luxury.

They help protect your physical and emotional wellbeing, lower the risk of burnout, and let you show up steadier for your child and the rest of your family. Rest is part of sustainable caregiving, not a reward for it.

Can respite care in Nebraska be paid for?

It sometimes can. Respite is a service that many Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers help pay for, and some states also run family-support or respite-subsidy programs outside of Medicaid.

Programs, eligibility, and covered hours are set at the state level and can change, so the reliable way to learn what is available to your family right now is to contact your Nebraska developmental-disabilities agency, your Medicaid office, or a state respite coalition.

How do families find respite providers?

There are several common paths. If your child is enrolled in a Medicaid waiver, a service coordinator can help you connect with approved respite providers. Families also find help through disability agencies, faith communities, respite coalitions and registries, and trusted people already in their circle, such as extended family or experienced sitters.

Some programs let you hire and direct your own respite worker. Ask your state agency which options apply to your situation.

Where do I start if I have never used respite before?

Start by naming what kind of break would help most, then reach out to your Nebraska developmental-disabilities agency or Medicaid office to ask whether your child qualifies for respite through a waiver or a state family-support program.

Ask to be screened, ask what interest or waiting lists exist, and keep a written record of who you spoke with and when. A local parent center or respite coalition can also walk you through your options for free.
