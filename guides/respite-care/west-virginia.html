A practical parent's guide

Respite care for families raising a child with disabilities in West Virginia

What respite is, why a break matters for your wellbeing, the ways it may be funded, and how West Virginia families find providers - explained gently, for parents who rarely get a moment to breathe.

Apply for help Official resources

What respite care is

Respite care is short-term, temporary care for your child so that you, the person who gives care every day, can step away and rest. That break might be a couple of hours to run errands or sleep, an afternoon, an overnight, or a longer stretch. It can happen in your own home with a caregiver who comes to you, at a provider's location, or in a community or camp setting, depending on what fits your child and what is available near you.

The idea behind respite is simple and humane. Caregiving for a child with disabilities is often around the clock, and no one can pour from an empty cup forever. Respite is the planned pause that lets you refill, so that the care your child depends on can continue steadily over the years ahead.

Rest is not stepping away from your child. It is part of how you keep showing up for them, week after week, year after year.

Why respite matters for caregiver wellbeing

When people picture support for a child with disabilities, they often think of therapies and equipment for the child. Respite is different: it is a support aimed squarely at the caregiver, and that makes it easy to overlook and easy to feel guilty about. It should be neither.

Without regular breaks, caregivers commonly face exhaustion, physical strain, anxiety, and the deep isolation that comes from rarely leaving the house. Over time that wears down health, relationships, and the ability to keep going. Respite gives you room to sleep, see a doctor, spend one-on-one time with your other children, nurture a marriage or friendship, or simply be a person for an afternoon. Those things are not extras. They are what allow a family to stay strong enough to care for the long haul.

You are allowed to need a break. Asking for respite is not a sign that you love your child any less. It is one of the most responsible things a caregiver can do.

How respite may be funded

Families do not always have to pay for respite entirely out of pocket. Respite is one of the supports most commonly offered through Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers, which use Medicaid funding to help children live and grow at home instead of in an institution. Many states also run their own family-support or caregiver-support programs that can help cover or arrange respite.

Here is the important part: what is offered, who qualifies, how many hours are approved, and how providers get paid are all decided at the state level. There is no single national rulebook, and the details in West Virginia are set by the state's own agencies. Because of that, the only way to know what your family can access is to ask the state directly rather than to rely on numbers you find online.

To learn what applies to you, reach out to West Virginia's Medicaid office and the state agency that handles developmental and intellectual disability services, and ask specifically about respite. If your child already has a waiver or a service coordinator, start there and ask whether respite is part of the plan and how to request it.

Please do not go by amounts or rules you read online

Respite eligibility, the number of hours allowed, and how funding works change over time and are specific to each program. Use this guide to understand the shape of what respite is and where to look, then confirm every detail directly with West Virginia's Medicaid office, disability services agency, or a state respite coalition.

How families find respite providers

Finding the right person or program can take a few tries, and that is normal. These are the general paths families use to find respite, and it often helps to pursue more than one at a time:

  • Through a waiver service coordinator or case manager. If your child has a Medicaid waiver, your coordinator can explain how respite is authorized and help connect you with approved providers.
  • Through respite agencies. Some agencies employ and train respite workers and match them to families, handling background checks and scheduling.
  • Through a state or local respite coalition and disability nonprofits. Many states have a respite network or lifespan respite program, along with parent groups that keep lists of trusted providers.
  • By hiring and directing your own caregiver. Some programs let families choose their own qualified worker, which can mean a relative, neighbor, or trusted friend who meets the program's requirements.
  • Through your child's care team and Parent Center. Doctors, therapists, and your federally funded state Parent Center often know local options and can point you in the right direction.

When you do find a provider, take the time to introduce them slowly, share your child's routines and medical needs in writing, and trust your instincts. A good respite relationship is built on comfort and safety for both your child and you.

Arranging respite while managing everything else can feel like one more heavy task. Go one step at a time, lean on the people who know your child's needs, and remember that the goal is not perfection. It is a real, restoring break for a caregiver who has more than earned one.

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

What is respite care?

Respite care is short-term, temporary care for your child that gives you, the primary caregiver, a break. It can last a few hours, a full day, an overnight, or longer, and it can happen in your own home, at a provider's location, or in a community setting.

The purpose is simple: to make sure the person who gives care can also rest, recover, and keep going.

Why does respite matter for our family's wellbeing?

Caring for a child with disabilities is a labor of love, and it is also constant. Without breaks, caregivers face exhaustion, isolation, and burnout, which can affect their health and the whole family.

Regular respite helps caregivers rest, tend to their own needs, spend time with other children, and stay well enough to keep caring over the long haul. It is not a luxury; it is part of sustainable caregiving.

How might respite care be paid for in West Virginia?

Respite is a common support offered through Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers, and some states also run family-support or caregiver-support programs that can help.

What is available, who qualifies, and how hours are approved are set at the state level. To learn what applies to your family, contact West Virginia's Medicaid office and developmental or intellectual disabilities agency, and ask specifically about respite.

How do families find respite providers?

Families commonly find respite through a Medicaid waiver service coordinator or case manager, through agencies that employ trained respite workers, and through their state or local respite coalition and disability nonprofits.

Some programs also allow families to hire and direct their own qualified caregiver. Ask your state agency, your child's care team, and your local Parent Center where to start.

Who can provide respite for my child?

It depends on the program and your child's needs. Respite may be provided by a trained worker from an agency, an independent caregiver you choose, or in some programs a qualified family member or trusted person.

Any funded arrangement usually has to meet the program's training and safety requirements, so confirm the rules with the program before you count on a particular person.
