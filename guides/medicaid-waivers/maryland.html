A practical parent's guide

Medicaid waivers for children with disabilities in Maryland

What Medicaid home and community-based waivers are, what they typically help with, why waitlists happen, and the honest first steps a Maryland family can take to begin.

Apply for help Official resources

What a Medicaid waiver is

If you are raising a child with a disability in Maryland, you have probably heard the word "waiver" and wondered what it actually means for your family. In plain terms, a Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waiver is a program that lets Medicaid pay for supports that help a person live at home and in their community, rather than in an institution. The name comes from the fact that these programs "waive" some of the usual Medicaid rules so that more children and families can get help where they live.

Waivers are authorized under federal law but run by each state, and that distinction is the key to understanding everything else. The federal government sets the broad framework and approves each program. Maryland decides which specific waivers to offer, who is eligible, which services are covered, and how many people each program can serve. That is why the details vary so much from state to state, and why the most reliable answers for your family will always come from Maryland's own Medicaid and developmental disabilities agencies.

A waiver is not a loophole or a favor. It is an established public program built on the simple idea that children do best at home, with their families, in their own communities.

What waivers typically help with

Every waiver is different, and the exact menu of services depends on the specific program a child is enrolled in. Still, across states, HCBS waivers tend to help with a recognizable set of supports. Understanding these categories in general terms can help you ask better questions when you contact the state.

  • Respite care. Short-term, trained care that gives family caregivers a needed break, whether for a few hours or a longer stretch.
  • Therapies. Services such as physical, occupational, and speech therapy, sometimes beyond what standard insurance covers.
  • Adaptive and medical equipment. Help toward devices and modifications that support daily living and mobility.
  • Personal care and in-home support. Hands-on assistance with daily activities, and help that supports a child's independence at home.
  • Service coordination. A coordinator or case manager who helps a family understand options and connect the pieces of a plan.

Because covered services differ by program, treat this list as a starting point for conversation, not a promise. When you speak with the state, ask specifically which services a given Maryland waiver includes and how a child accesses them once enrolled.

Why waitlists are common

Here is an honest part that catches many families off guard: waivers usually serve a limited number of people, and demand often outpaces the available slots. As a result, many states maintain a waiting list or a registry, and a child may spend time on that list before services begin. This is a normal feature of how waivers work, not a sign that anything has gone wrong, and it is not a denial.

Because time on a list can matter, the practical takeaway is simple: reach out and get your child registered as early as you reasonably can, even if you are still gathering information. Ask the state how its list or registry works, whether there is any process for urgent or emergency needs, and what you can do in the meantime. Getting your name in the system is often the single most important early step.

How to begin, step by step

The process can feel opaque from the outside, but it usually starts with a few concrete actions. You do not need to have everything figured out before you make the first call.

  1. Contact the state. Reach out to the Maryland Medicaid program or the state developmental disabilities agency and explain that you are seeking waiver services for your child.
  2. Ask for a screening. Request a waiver screening or intake, and ask to be added to any relevant registry or waiting list right away.
  3. Ask which programs may fit. Different waivers serve different needs. Ask which programs your child may be eligible for and how eligibility is determined.
  4. Gather documentation. Ask what records help, such as diagnosis and evaluation paperwork, and keep copies of everything you send and receive.
  5. Get free guidance. A federally funded Parent Center or a local service coordinator can walk you through the steps at no cost.

One reassuring note on money: many waivers look at the child's situation rather than only whole-household income, which is why some children qualify for waiver help even when a family would not qualify for standard Medicaid. Do not rule yourself out on income alone. Ask the state how eligibility is decided for the specific program.

A note on specifics

Waiver names, covered services, income rules, and waitlist details change over time and are set at the state level. This guide stays general on purpose. For anything specific to your child, confirm the current details directly with the Maryland Medicaid or developmental disabilities agency, or with a Parent Center that serves your area.

Navigating waivers takes patience, and you should not have to do it alone. If your family is caught in a gap while you wait, or you need a hand understanding your options, Teagan's Crown is here to help however we can.

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

What is a Medicaid HCBS waiver?

A Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waiver is a Medicaid program, authorized under federal law and run by each state, that lets Medicaid pay for supports that help a person live at home and in the community instead of in an institution.

For families, that can mean services like respite, personal care, therapies, and equipment. Because waivers are run at the state level, the specific programs and rules are set by Maryland, so confirm current details with the state Medicaid or developmental disabilities agency.

What do Medicaid waivers typically help with?

Waivers vary, but they commonly help with supports such as respite care, in-home personal care and support, certain therapies, and adaptive or medical equipment, along with service coordination that helps a family navigate the system.

The exact list of covered services depends on the specific Maryland waiver a child is enrolled in, so ask the state agency which services a given program includes.

Why are there waitlists for Medicaid waivers?

Waivers serve a limited number of people, and demand often exceeds the available slots, so many states maintain waiting lists or registries. Being placed on a list is a normal part of the process and is not a denial.

It is important to apply and get on any relevant registry as early as possible, because time on the list can matter. Ask the state agency how its list works and whether any urgent-need process applies to your child.

How do we start applying for a Medicaid waiver in Maryland?

A good first step is to contact the Maryland Medicaid program or the state developmental disabilities agency and ask for a waiver screening or how to be added to the relevant registry. They can explain eligibility, which programs may fit your child, and what documentation to gather.

A federally funded Parent Center or a local service coordinator can also guide you at no cost.

Does my family income determine whether my child qualifies?

Many HCBS waivers consider the child's situation rather than only whole-household income, which is why some children qualify for waiver services when they would not qualify for standard Medicaid on family income alone.

Rules differ by program, so do not assume your family earns too much to apply. Ask the Maryland Medicaid or developmental disabilities agency how eligibility is determined for the specific waiver.
