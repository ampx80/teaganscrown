Medicaid waivers for children with disabilities in Virginia

What a Medicaid waiver actually is, what it can help pay for, why waitlists happen, and the honest, general steps a Virginia family can take to get started.

Apply for help Official resources

What a Medicaid waiver is

If you are raising a child with a disability in Virginia, you have probably heard the phrase "Medicaid waiver" and wondered what it really means for your family. In plain terms, a Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waiver is a Medicaid program that helps a person with a disability get care and support at home and in the community, rather than in a hospital or institution. It is authorized under federal law and run by each state, which means the broad idea is national but the details are decided state by state.

The word "waiver" points to what makes these programs special. They waive, or set aside, some of the usual Medicaid rules. In many states that includes the way income is counted, so a child may be able to qualify based on the child's own situation and level of need rather than the whole family's finances. That single feature is why so many families who assumed they earned too much for Medicaid discover that a waiver can still open a door for their child.

A waiver is not a favor or a handout. It is a support your child may be entitled to, designed so families can raise their children where children belong: at home.

What waivers typically help with

Exactly what a waiver covers depends on the state and on the specific waiver, and the list changes over time. Speaking generally, HCBS waivers across the country tend to help with things like:

  • Respite care, which gives family caregivers a needed break while a trained person cares for their child.
  • Personal care and in-home support, helping with daily activities like bathing, dressing, eating, and getting around the house.
  • Therapies and related services that support a child's development and health.
  • Assistive technology and adaptive equipment, the tools and devices that help a child communicate, move, and take part in daily life.
  • Service coordination, meaning a person who helps you understand options, build a plan, and connect the pieces.

Not every waiver covers every one of these, and each has its own limits and rules. Treat the list above as a general picture of what waivers are for, and ask the Virginia agency or your assigned coordinator for the current, specific details that apply to your child.

Why waitlists are common

Here is the honest part. Waivers serve a set number of people, so when more families apply than there are available slots, states place eligible children on a waiting list until a slot opens. Waitlists are common across many states, and a wait can be significant. This is one of the hardest realities for families to hear, especially when a child's needs feel urgent right now.

Because of that, the common advice is simple: start early. Request a screening and get your child on any applicable list as soon as you can, since time on a list often matters. While you wait, keep working with your care team, look into other supports, and do not put your life on hold waiting for a single answer. For how Virginia's lists and priorities work at this moment, go straight to the state agency rather than relying on secondhand accounts, because these details do change.

General steps to begin in Virginia

Every family's path looks a little different, but the starting points are usually the same. Here is a general roadmap you can adapt.

1. Contact the state agency and ask for a screening

The first move is to reach out to Virginia's Medicaid program or its developmental disabilities agency and ask about a waiver screening or intake for your child. This is the conversation where someone explains which waivers might fit, how eligibility is decided, and what happens next. If you are not sure which office to call, your local Parent Center or your child's care team can point you in the right direction.

2. Gather your documentation

Screenings and applications usually ask for records about your child's diagnosis, needs, and daily support. Pulling together medical records, evaluations, and any therapy or school documents ahead of time makes the process smoother. Keep copies of everything you submit and note the dates, names, and reference numbers from each conversation.

3. Complete the screening and get on any list

A coordinator will assess your child's eligibility and level of need. If your child qualifies and a slot is not immediately available, ask to be placed on the appropriate waiting list and confirm your child's status in writing when you can. Ask what to expect next and how you will be contacted.

4. Ask questions and use your supports

You do not have to navigate this alone. Ask the agency to explain anything that is unclear, lean on your local Parent Center for free guidance, and connect with other Virginia families who have walked this road. If you hit a wall or a wait, keep looking at other funding and community supports at the same time.

A note on specifics

Program names, eligibility rules, covered services, and waitlist details are set by the state and change over time. This guide stays general on purpose so it will not steer you wrong. For the accurate, current specifics that apply to your child, always confirm with the official Virginia Medicaid and developmental disabilities sources linked below.

When a waiver is not enough, or not yet

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

What is a Medicaid HCBS waiver?

A Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waiver is a Medicaid program that helps people with disabilities receive care and support in their own home and community instead of in an institution. It is authorized under federal law and run by each state.

Waivers can help cover services and supports that standard Medicaid may not, and in many states they also let a child qualify based on the child's own situation rather than the whole family's income. The specific waivers, rules, and covered services differ by state, so Virginia families should confirm the current details with the state's Medicaid and developmental disabilities agencies.

What do Medicaid waivers typically help pay for?

It varies by state and by the specific waiver, but common categories include respite care that gives family caregivers a break, personal care and in-home support, certain therapies, assistive technology and adaptive equipment, and help coordinating services.

Not every waiver covers every service, and covered items and limits change over time. Ask the state agency or your assigned coordinator for the current list of what a given Virginia waiver covers.

Why are there waitlists for Medicaid waivers?

Waivers serve a set number of people, so when more families apply than there are available slots, states place eligible children on a waiting list until a slot opens. Waitlists are common across many states.

Because a wait can be long, families are usually encouraged to request a screening and get on any list as early as possible, and to look at other supports in the meantime. Contact the Virginia agency for how its lists work right now.

How do we start a Medicaid waiver in Virginia?

The general first step is to contact the state Medicaid or developmental disabilities agency and ask for a waiver screening or intake. A coordinator will explain which waivers may fit your child, how eligibility is determined, and what documentation to gather.

Because processes and program names change, start with the official Virginia Medicaid and developmental disabilities websites rather than secondhand information, and ask your child's care team and local Parent Center to help you navigate the steps.

Does my family income disqualify my child from a waiver?

Not necessarily. One reason HCBS waivers matter is that many states let children qualify based on the child's own eligibility and level of need rather than the entire family's income, which can open a door that regular Medicaid does not.

Rules differ by state and by waiver, so do not assume your child is ineligible. Ask the Virginia agency to walk you through how eligibility is actually determined before ruling anything out.
