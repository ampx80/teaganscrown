A practical parent's guide

Disability benefits and ABLE accounts for children in Maine

How SSI works, how Medicaid often connects to it, and how ABLE accounts let your family save without putting benefits at risk - explained in plain language for Maine families.

Apply for help Official resources

When you are raising a child with a disability, the word "benefits" can feel like a locked door with no key. There is a federal baseline that every family in Maine can build from, and understanding its three main pieces makes the whole system less overwhelming. This guide walks through Supplemental Security Income, how Medicaid often connects to it, and ABLE accounts that let you save for the future without losing the support your child depends on today.

You do not have to understand every rule to begin. You only have to take the first honest step, and let the official agencies confirm what fits your child.

SSI: a monthly foundation for children

Supplemental Security Income, or SSI, is a federal program run by the Social Security Administration. It provides monthly cash payments to people with limited income and resources who are aged, blind, or have a disability, and that includes children. For a family, an SSI payment can help cover the everyday costs of raising a child whose needs stretch a household budget in ways others may never see.

A child can qualify for SSI when they have a qualifying disability and the family's income and resources fall within the program's limits. Because SSI is needs-based, the Social Security Administration looks at the family's financial situation as part of deciding eligibility for a child. The exact income limits and the size of the monthly payment are set at the federal level and can change over time, so the reliable way to learn what applies to your child is to apply through the Social Security Administration and let them make the determination. Do not rule your family out based on a number you read somewhere online.

How Medicaid often connects to SSI

One of the most valuable things about SSI is what often comes with it. In many states, a child who is approved for SSI also becomes eligible for Medicaid, and in a number of states that Medicaid coverage starts automatically once SSI is approved. Medicaid can open the door to health coverage, therapies, and services that private insurance may not fully cover.

Maine administers its own Medicaid program, known locally as MaineCare, so the precise way SSI and Medicaid connect is decided at the state level. That means the smartest move is to confirm the details directly with your state Medicaid office rather than assuming your child is or is not covered. Ask specifically whether an SSI approval brings Medicaid with it, and whether there is any separate application step your family needs to take. There may also be other Medicaid pathways for children with disabilities that do not depend on SSI at all, which is another good question to raise.

Two agencies, two conversations

SSI is federal, run by the Social Security Administration. Medicaid is run by your state. These are separate offices with separate applications, even though they often work together. Keeping them straight, and keeping a written record of each call, will save you real frustration later.

ABLE accounts: saving without losing benefits

Here is the worry so many families carry: if we set money aside for our child, will it disqualify them from the benefits they need? For needs-based programs like SSI and Medicaid, ordinary savings can count against strict resource limits, which has historically punished families for planning ahead. The federal ABLE Act was created to fix exactly this problem.

An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings and investment account for eligible people with disabilities. Money held in an ABLE account is generally not counted against the resource limits for benefits like SSI and Medicaid, within federal rules. That means a family can build a cushion for disability-related expenses, from equipment to education to daily living costs, without automatically putting those benefits at risk. Earnings in the account can grow tax-free when used for qualified disability expenses.

Maine runs or partners on an ABLE program, so residents have a way to open an ABLE account. Because plans differ and their rules, fees, and features can change, and because a family may also open an eligible plan offered through another state, always check the official state ABLE plan for current details before you enroll. The plan's own website is the authoritative place to confirm contribution limits, balance rules, and how to open an account.

Where to start

You do not have to do all of this at once. A calm, in-order approach works best:

  1. Apply for SSI through the Social Security Administration, and let them determine your child's eligibility rather than guessing.
  2. Ask about Medicaid at your state Medicaid office, including whether an SSI approval brings coverage and what other pathways exist for children with disabilities.
  3. Explore an ABLE account through the official state ABLE plan once you understand your child's benefits, so your savings work with those benefits, not against them.
  4. Keep records of every call, name, and date. This one habit makes follow-up far easier when something needs a second conversation.

These federal frameworks, SSI, Medicaid, and the ABLE Act, are the same building blocks families rely on across the country. What changes from state to state is the fine print, which is exactly why the official agencies are the ones to confirm your child's specifics.

None of this is a substitute for the official word, and it is not personalized financial or legal advice. Think of it as a map of the landscape, so that when you pick up the phone with the SSA, your state Medicaid office, or the ABLE plan, you already know the shape of what you are asking about.

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

What is SSI, and can a child qualify?

SSI, or Supplemental Security Income, is a federal program run by the Social Security Administration that provides monthly cash payments to people with limited income and resources who are aged, blind, or have a disability.

Children can qualify when they have a qualifying disability and the family's income and resources fall within the program's limits. Because those limits and the exact payment can change, apply through the Social Security Administration and let them determine eligibility for your child.

How does Medicaid connect to SSI in Maine?

In many states, a child who is approved for SSI is also eligible for Medicaid, and in a number of states that Medicaid coverage begins automatically.

Maine administers its own Medicaid program, known as MaineCare, so the exact way SSI and Medicaid connect is set at the state level. Contact your state Medicaid office to confirm how coverage works for your child and whether any separate step is needed.

What is an ABLE account?

An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings account created under the federal ABLE Act for eligible people with disabilities. It lets a family save and invest for disability-related expenses.

Money held in the account is generally not counted against the resource limits for benefits like SSI and Medicaid, within federal rules, so families can build a cushion without automatically putting those benefits at risk. Confirm current contribution and balance rules with the official ABLE plan.

Does Maine have an ABLE program?

Maine runs or partners on an ABLE program so that residents can open an ABLE account. Because plans, fees, and features can change, and because a family may also open an eligible plan offered through another state, check the official state ABLE plan for current details before you enroll.

The plan's own website is the authoritative place to confirm how to open an account and what it offers.

Will saving money cause my child to lose benefits?

Ordinary savings can count against the resource limits for needs-based benefits like SSI and Medicaid, which is one reason families worry about setting money aside. An ABLE account is designed to address exactly this, since funds in it are generally not counted within federal limits.

The safest approach is to confirm the current rules directly with the Social Security Administration, your state Medicaid office, and the official ABLE plan before making large deposits.
