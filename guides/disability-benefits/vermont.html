A practical parent's guide

Disability benefits and ABLE accounts for children in Vermont

How SSI works for children, how Medicaid often connects to it, and how ABLE accounts let your family save for the future without putting benefits at risk - in plain language for busy Vermont families.

Apply for help Official resources

Start with the federal baseline

When you are raising a child with a disability, the words for the help that exists can feel like an alphabet you never asked to learn. The good news is that a lot of the foundation is federal, which means the shape of it is the same in Vermont as it is anywhere else. Three pieces form that baseline: SSI, Medicaid, and ABLE accounts. Vermont then runs and administers its own programs on top of that federal frame, so your final answers come from state offices, but the map below is a place to begin.

Think of this guide as a way to understand how the pieces fit together. It will not give you exact dollar figures or income limits, because those are set by the agencies and can change. What it will do is help you walk into a phone call already knowing what to ask for.

You do not have to master the whole system in a weekend. You just have to learn the next right question to ask.

SSI: monthly support for a child

Supplemental Security Income, or SSI, is a federal program run by the Social Security Administration. It provides monthly cash payments to people with disabilities who have limited income and resources, and children can qualify. For a child, there are two parts to eligibility: a disability standard that looks at how the child's condition affects daily life, and a financial test. Because a child lives with their family, some of the parents' income and resources are counted in that test, a process often described as "deeming."

The exact income limits and payment amounts are set at the federal level and are adjusted over time, so please do not rely on a number you read secondhand. The Social Security Administration is the one place to confirm what applies to your child right now, and it is worth calling even if you are unsure you qualify.

How Medicaid often connects

Medicaid is health coverage that many children with disabilities depend on for doctors, therapies, equipment, and more. It is built on a federal framework but run by each state, and Vermont administers its own Medicaid program. The important link to know is this: in many states, a child who is approved for SSI also gains access to Medicaid, so the two often travel together.

SSI is not the only door, though. There are disability-related pathways to Medicaid that do not depend on receiving SSI, and the rules for how they work are set by the state. If your child does not qualify for SSI, that does not automatically mean the door to Medicaid is closed. The right move is to ask Vermont's Medicaid office directly which pathway fits your child.

ABLE accounts: saving without losing benefits

One of the hardest binds families face is that benefits like SSI and Medicaid have strict limits on how much a person can have in savings. For years that meant setting money aside for your child could actually threaten the help they were counting on. The ABLE Act was written to fix exactly that problem.

An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings and investment account for eligible people with disabilities. Money in it grows tax-free when used for qualified disability expenses, and balances within the limits set in law generally do not count against the resource limits for benefits like SSI and Medicaid. In other words, an ABLE account lets a family save for a child's future without automatically jeopardizing the benefits they rely on today. Eligibility generally depends on the disability having begun before an age set in federal law, so confirm whether your child qualifies.

Vermont runs or partners on an ABLE program so that residents have an official plan to open an account through. Many plans also accept out-of-state residents, so it is worth comparing options. Start with the official state ABLE plan to see the current fees, features, and enrollment steps.

Please do not go by numbers you read online

Income limits, resource limits, benefit amounts, and ABLE plan details change over time and are set by the agencies that run them. The only reliable sources for what applies to your child right now are the Social Security Administration, Vermont's Medicaid office, and the official state ABLE plan. Use this guide to understand how the pieces fit, then confirm every figure directly.

None of this is meant to be done in a single afternoon. Getting on the phone once, asking one clear question, and writing down what you hear is real progress. You are allowed to take it one step at a time.

Questions families ask

Answers, in plain language.

What is SSI, and can a child receive it?

SSI, or Supplemental Security Income, is a federal program run by the Social Security Administration that pays monthly cash benefits to people with disabilities who have limited income and resources, and children can qualify.

For a child, there is both a disability standard and a financial test, and because the child lives with family, part of the parents' income and resources is counted. Rules and dollar amounts are set at the federal level and can change, so confirm the current figures directly with the Social Security Administration.

How does Medicaid connect to SSI?

In many states a child who is approved for SSI gains access to Medicaid, and there are also disability-related pathways to Medicaid that do not depend on SSI.

Medicaid is run by each state within a federal framework, and Vermont administers its own Medicaid program. Because the exact link between SSI and Medicaid, and the other ways in, are set by the state, confirm how it works for your child with Vermont's Medicaid office.

What is an ABLE account?

An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings and investment account for eligible people with disabilities, created by the federal ABLE Act. Money saved in it grows tax-free when used for qualified disability expenses.

Balances within the limits set in law generally do not count against the resource limits for benefits like SSI and Medicaid, which lets a family save for the future without putting current benefits at risk. Confirm eligibility and account details with the official state ABLE plan.

Does Vermont have its own ABLE program?

Vermont runs or partners on an ABLE program so that residents have an official plan to open an account through. In many cases a family can also open an ABLE account through another state's plan, since most plans accept out-of-state residents.

Start with the official state ABLE plan to compare fees, features, and how to enroll, and confirm the current details before you open an account.

Will saving money cost my child their benefits?

That fear is exactly what ABLE accounts were designed to ease. Because qualifying savings inside an ABLE account, up to the limits set in law, are not counted the way ordinary savings are, families can set money aside for a child's needs without automatically jeopardizing SSI or Medicaid.

Because the specific limits and rules can change, confirm them with the Social Security Administration and the official state ABLE plan before relying on any figure.
